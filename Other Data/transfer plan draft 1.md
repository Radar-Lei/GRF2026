Technology Transfer Plan: A Strategic Pathway from GRF Discovery to ITF-Funded Commercialisation1. Strategic Context and Innovation PathwayThis Technology Transfer Plan outlines a structured, multi-phase strategy to translate the fundamental research discoveries from this General Research Fund (GRF) project into tangible socio-economic impact for Hong Kong. It serves as a strategic bridge, connecting the foundational knowledge generated under the Research Grants Council (RGC) with the applied research, development, and commercialisation ecosystem supported by the Hong Kong Innovation and Technology Fund (ITF). This plan demonstrates foresight by mapping a clear and viable trajectory for the project's outcomes, ensuring that the initial public investment in fundamental research is leveraged to its full potential, ultimately contributing to Hong Kong's competitiveness and long-term development.1.1 The RGC-ITF Funding Continuum: A Strategic OverviewHong Kong's public research funding architecture is strategically bifurcated: the RGC champions the creation of new knowledge through fundamental research, while the ITF, administered by the Innovation and Technology Commission (ITC), is mandated to finance projects that upgrade local industries and promote innovation.1 This plan is designed to navigate this continuum seamlessly. It presents the RGC-funded project not as an endpoint, but as the critical first stage of a larger innovation lifecycle.By presenting a well-defined pathway toward future ITF support, this plan provides the RGC assessment panel with a compelling narrative of broader impact. It preemptively addresses the crucial question of the research's long-term value beyond academic publications. This approach signals a commitment to the responsible stewardship of public funds, demonstrating an understanding that fundamental discoveries are the seed capital for future technological advancements and economic growth. A GRF proposal that contains a credible and detailed plan for downstream development and commercialisation is inherently more robust, as it presents a complete vision from initial discovery to eventual deployment, aligning perfectly with the government's overarching goal of fostering a vibrant innovation ecosystem.31.2 Alignment with HKSAR’s Innovation and Technology Development BlueprintThe proposed research is strategically aligned with the priorities articulated in the Hong Kong Innovation and Technology Development Blueprint. This foundational government document outlines key strategic areas for development, including life and health technology, artificial intelligence (AI) and data science, advanced manufacturing, and new energy technologies.1 This project's focus on directly contributes to the pillar of [e.g., "life and health technology"], addressing a critical area of focus for the HKSAR government.This alignment is not a superficial exercise; it positions the project's future outputs within a powerful political and economic framework. The government has committed substantial capital, such as the HK$5 billion injection for the Research, Academic and Industry Sectors One-plus (RAISe+) Scheme, to accelerate progress in these strategic domains.4 ITF funding schemes are the primary instruments for executing this policy, with a clear emphasis on promoting "new industrialisation" and developing advanced manufacturing capabilities.5 By explicitly linking this fundamental research to these strategic goals from its inception, the project is positioned as a future solution to a problem the government has already identified as critical and is prepared to fund. This creates a significant "pull" effect, enhancing the likelihood of success for subsequent ITF applications, as they will be evaluated not in isolation, but as contributors to a pre-approved, high-priority government mission.2. Intellectual Property (IP) Management and Asset DevelopmentA proactive and commercially astute approach to Intellectual Property management is the cornerstone of this Technology Transfer Plan. The IP generated from this research is the core asset that will enable all subsequent development and commercialisation activities. The following protocol ensures that this asset is identified, protected, and strategically leveraged to attract downstream funding and partnerships.2.1 IP Generation and Protection ProtocolA systematic process for IP management will be implemented in close collaboration with the university's Technology Transfer Office (TTO).6 This protocol involves three key steps:Invention Disclosure and Assessment: The research team will hold formal, bi-annual review meetings with the TTO throughout the GRF project lifecycle. The purpose of these meetings is to proactively review research progress, data, and discoveries to identify potentially patentable inventions at the earliest possible stage.Prior Art and Patentability Analysis: Upon identification of a promising invention, a thorough prior art search will be commissioned through the TTO. This crucial step ensures the novelty and inventiveness of the discovery, establishing a strong foundation for a defensible patent and avoiding unnecessary expenditure on non-patentable subject matter.Strategic Filing Timeline: A provisional patent application is planned to be filed at a key project milestone, anticipated around Month 18 of the GRF project. This action secures an early priority date at a relatively low cost, providing a one-year window to gather further data to strengthen the patent claims. The filing of a full Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT) application will be timed to coincide with the conclusion of the GRF project. This ensures that a tangible, protected IP asset exists at the precise moment the project transitions towards seeking applied R&D funding from the ITF. For first-time patent applications, the team will explore support from the ITF's Patent Application Grant (PAG) to offset costs.72.2 Strategic IP Ownership Models for Downstream FundingThe IP strategy must be flexible and responsive to the specific requirements of different commercialisation pathways and ITF funding schemes. The ITF's rules regarding IP ownership are not merely administrative; they are powerful commercial incentives designed to de-risk investment for the private sector and encourage university-industry collaboration.8 This plan incorporates a decision-making framework for IP ownership based on the intended downstream funding mechanism.Scenario A: University-Led Development and Licensing: In the initial phases of applied R&D, or if the technology has broad applicability across multiple industries, the university will retain full ownership of the IP. This is the default model for schemes like the Innovation and Technology Support Programme (ITSP) Platform and Seed projects, where the lead applicant (the university) owns the resulting IP.8 This strategy preserves the university's ability to negotiate multiple non-exclusive licenses or a more lucrative exclusive license with the strongest commercial partner.Scenario B: Deep Industrial Partnership or Spin-out Venture: When a single, committed industry partner is essential for commercialisation, a more flexible IP model will be adopted. The ITSP (Collaborative) scheme is specifically designed for this scenario, allowing the industry co-applicant to own the project's IP, provided it contributes at least 50% of the total project cost.3 Understanding this provision transforms the dynamic of industry engagement. The conversation shifts from a simple request for sponsorship to a strategic business proposition: offering a corporate partner a government-subsidised pathway to acquire a valuable, de-risked technological asset. This significantly lowers the barrier to securing the substantial private investment required for late-stage development.3. A Phased Pathway to MarketThis section details a concrete, multi-year roadmap from the conclusion of the GRF project to market entry. The pathway is structured in three distinct phases, each with clear objectives, targeted activities, and alignment with a specific ITF funding scheme. This phased approach systematically de-risks the technology, builds value, and attracts increasing levels of investment and partnership over time.Phase I (Year 1 Post-GRF): Technology De-risking and Proof-of-Concept DevelopmentObjective: To bridge the gap between fundamental research findings and a tangible, demonstrable technology. The primary goal is to develop a working laboratory prototype or generate robust proof-of-concept data that validates the technology's core function and potential.Target ITF Scheme: Innovation and Technology Support Programme (ITSP) - Seed Project.8Strategic Rationale: The ITSP Seed stream is perfectly suited for this crucial, early stage of applied R&D. It is designed for projects that are "exploratory and forward-looking".10 Most importantly, industry sponsorship is not mandatory for Seed projects.8 This feature is critical, as it provides a vital funding bridge to mature the technology from a TRL (Technology Readiness Level) of 2-3 (typical for GRF outcomes) to a TRL of 4-5, where a functional prototype exists. Without this intermediate step, attracting an industry partner for later phases would be exceptionally difficult. The maximum funding of up to HK$1.4 million for university-led projects is sufficient to support the necessary personnel, materials, and testing for this de-risking phase.Phase II (Years 2-3 Post-GRF): Applied R&D and Industry PartnershipObjective: To secure a strategic industry partner to co-develop the technology for a specific market application. This phase focuses on scaling up the prototype, refining its design for manufacturing, and conducting validation under industry-relevant conditions.Target ITF Schemes: Innovation and Technology Support Programme (ITSP) - Collaborative Project or Platform Project.8Strategic Rationale: The success of Phase I is a direct prerequisite for Phase II. The tangible proof-of-concept developed under the ITSP-Seed grant serves as the primary tool for attracting a corporate partner.ITSP (Collaborative): This is the preferred route for deep collaboration with a single, committed partner. It requires the company to contribute at least 50% of the total project cost, ensuring genuine "skin in the game" and aligning the partner's interests with the project's success.8 As discussed in Section 2.2, the ability to offer the partner ownership of the resulting IP is a powerful incentive to secure this level of investment.ITSP (Platform): This route is more appropriate if the technology has broad applications across an industry sector and a single exclusive partner is not desirable. It requires a lower industry sponsorship threshold (at least 10% of the project cost), which can be sourced from one or more companies.9 The university retains IP ownership, allowing for a wider licensing strategy post-project.Phase III (Years 4+ Post-GRF): Commercialisation and Market EntryObjective: To facilitate the final transition of the co-developed technology from the laboratory into the market. This phase outlines two distinct, potentially parallel, pathways to maximize the probability of a successful commercial outcome.Pathway A: Validation and LicensingTarget ITF Scheme: Public Sector Trial Scheme (PSTS).11Strategic Rationale: The PSTS provides funding for the production of prototypes and samples for trial use within the Hong Kong public sector (e.g., government departments, public hospitals, statutory bodies). A successful trial under the PSTS serves as an invaluable, impartial, third-party validation of the technology's performance and utility. This high-profile case study significantly de-risks the technology for potential commercial licensees, strengthens the business case, and can command a higher licensing fee or royalty rate.Pathway B: New Venture Creation (Spin-out)Target ITF Scheme: Technology Start-up Support Scheme for Universities (TSSSU).13Strategic Rationale: If the technology is disruptive, constitutes a platform with multiple products, or if a suitable licensee cannot be identified, the project team will pursue the formation of a university spin-out company. The TSSSU is designed specifically for this purpose, providing annual funding of up to HK100 million for promising university start-ups.4This dual-pathway strategy demonstrates adaptability and a mature understanding of the complexities of commercialisation, assuring assessors that multiple viable routes to impact have been considered.Table 1: ITF Funding Pathway and Decision MatrixScheme NameITSP (Seed) 8ITSP (Platform) 8ITSP (Collaborative) 3PSTS 12TSSSU 13RAISe+ 44. Stakeholder Engagement and Dissemination StrategySuccessful technology transfer is contingent upon building a strong network of partners, collaborators, and end-users. This requires a proactive engagement and dissemination strategy that begins during the GRF project and continues through every phase of commercialisation. This approach mitigates the primary risk of academic technology transfer: developing a technology in isolation that fails to meet a real-world market need.5.1 Industry and R&D Centre Engagement PlanThe ITF framework is built upon collaboration between academia, industry, and Hong Kong's R&D Centres (e.g., LSCM, ASTRI).15 This plan includes a structured approach to building these critical relationships early.Target Partner Identification: The project team has identified several profiles of ideal industry partners, including:Multinational corporations in the [e.g., pharmaceutical or medical device] sector with R&D or business development offices in Hong Kong.Local small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the [e.g., precision engineering or biotech services] industry looking to upgrade their technological capabilities.Venture capital firms specializing in early-stage deep technology investments.Proactive Engagement Activities: Engagement will not wait until the technology is fully developed. A timeline of activities will be implemented starting in Year 2 of the GRF project:Industry Events: Presenting preliminary findings and the technology's vision at key industry conferences and trade shows, such as InnoEX, to gain visibility and solicit initial feedback.5Targeted Outreach: Initiating exploratory, non-confidential discussions with R&D heads and corporate strategy leaders at target companies to understand their needs and challenges.R&D Centre Collaboration: Engaging with relevant R&D Centres to leverage their deep industry networks, technical facilities, and expertise in applied research. This builds a foundation for potential future joint applications for ITF funding.This early and continuous dialogue serves as a form of market validation, allowing the research and subsequent development to be guided by real-world needs. It transforms the eventual search for an ITSP sponsor from a series of cold calls into a continuation of established relationships, dramatically increasing the probability of securing a committed partner.5.2 Dissemination Beyond Academic PublicationsWhile peer-reviewed publications are essential for validating the science, they are insufficient for effective technology transfer. The dissemination strategy must also target non-academic audiences, including potential investors, partners, and end-users.18Industry-Facing Collateral: The research team will develop accessible dissemination materials, including:Technical White Papers: Summarizing the technology's capabilities, performance data, and competitive advantages in a format suitable for industry engineers and product managers.Business Case Summaries: One-page documents outlining the market opportunity, value proposition, and potential ROI for investors and corporate executives.Interactive Demonstrations:Webinars and Workshops: Upon development of the prototype in Phase I, the team will host targeted webinars for an international audience and hands-on workshops for local stakeholders to demonstrate the technology's functionality and gather user feedback.Continuous Dialogue with Funding Bodies: The project team will maintain regular communication with the university TTO and will seek opportunities for informal consultation with program managers at the ITC. This ensures that future ITF proposals are optimally structured and aligned with the latest funding priorities and assessment criteria.6. ConclusionThis Technology Transfer Plan presents a comprehensive, realistic, and strategically sound roadmap for translating the outputs of the proposed GRF research into significant and lasting impact. The strength of this plan lies in its integrated approach, which demonstrates not only scientific excellence but also a sophisticated understanding of Hong Kong's innovation and commercialisation ecosystem.Key strategic advantages of this plan include:Strategic Alignment: The plan is explicitly aligned with the HKSAR Government's Innovation and Technology Development Blueprint and leverages the RGC-ITF funding continuum to maximize the return on public investment.Phased and Pragmatic Pathway: It outlines a logical, multi-phase progression that systematically de-risks the technology, builds value incrementally, and utilizes the most appropriate ITF scheme at each stage, from the non-sponsored ITSP-Seed grant to the industry-driven ITSP-Collaborative program.Flexible and Commercially-Minded IP Strategy: The plan incorporates a nuanced approach to IP ownership, using it as a powerful strategic tool to attract and secure the deep private sector partnerships necessary for commercial success.Proactive Stakeholder Engagement: By initiating dialogue with industry, R&D Centres, and end-users during the fundamental research phase, the plan mitigates market risk and builds the collaborative relationships essential for a successful transition from lab to market.By adopting this structured and forward-looking approach, this project is positioned not merely to advance scientific knowledge, but to generate new technologies, create high-value employment, and enhance the competitiveness of a key Hong Kong industry. This plan provides a clear and credible blueprint for achieving that vision.
