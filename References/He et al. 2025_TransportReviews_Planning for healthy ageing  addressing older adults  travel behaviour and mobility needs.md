# EDITORIAL

# Planning for healthy ageing: addressing older adults’ travel behaviour and mobility needs

Population ageing is a global phenomenon, yet planning efforts have often fallen short in addressing the mobility needs of this growing and vulnerable demographic. In the 2025 TRB Thomas B. Deen Distinguished Lecture, Professor Sandi Rosenbloom called for greater attention to the travel characteristics and needs of under-researched sociodemographic groups, particularly what she terms the “marginalized majority” – older adults and women. This demographic shift presents an urgent challenge, underscoring the need for urban environments that are more intentionally planned and designed to support the mobility needs of older adults. The World Health Organization’s “Global Strategy and Action Plan on Ageing and Health” (2017) also recognises the critical role of the built environment in enhancing the quality of life of older adults.

# 1. Understanding older adults’ travel behaviour and mobility needs

As cities adapt to demographic shifts, population ageing presents both challenges and opportunities for rethinking urban development and transportation systems. The United Nations emphasises the importance of maintaining physical, mental, and social well-being in later life, which is not only an aspirational goal for individuals but also a cornerstone of livable cities (Amuthavalli Thiyagarajan et al., 2022). However, many cities worldwide struggle to meet the needs of ageing populations due to the lack of supportive, accessible, and inclusive policies and designs (Buffel & Phillipson, 2016). The mismatch between urban environments and the needs of older adults creates barriers to their well-being and participation in community life.

# 1.1. Older adults’ travel behaviour, preferences, and challenges

Time use, mobility patterns, and social participation characteristics differ between older and younger adults (He et al., 2018; He et al., 2022; He & He, 2025a). Understanding the travel behaviour, needs and preferences of older adults is key to building age-friendly cities. Studies consistently show that older adults prioritise safety, accessibility and affordability in their travel choices (Alsnih & Hensher, 2003; De Paepe et al., 2023). For them, mobility is not just a matter of reaching a destination but also closely related to maintaining physical and mental health, fostering social connections, accessing essential services and enhancing quality of life (Cinderby et al., 2018).

However, older adults face unique mobility challenges. Limitations in physical functioning (e.g., declining physical strength and mobility) and cognitive deterioration (e.g., memory loss and diminishing spatial perception) make it doubly difficult for them to cope with urban environments designed for younger individuals (Clarke & Gallagher, 2013). Urban infrastructure deficiencies, such as insufficient seating, poorly timed traffic signals and inaccessible public transportation, further exacerbate mobility barriers (Wong et al., 2018). Furthermore, the digital divide excludes older adults who are unable to use smartphones, navigation apps (e.g., Google Maps), or other information and communications technology tools from the conveniences of modern transportation systems in smart cities (Wang et al., 2021).

# 1.2. Emerging transportation technologies: balancing new opportunities and risks

New transportation technologies, such as the shared micromobility services that have revolutionised urban transportation, may pose barriers and risks for older adults who travel on foot. In China, for example, the rapid deployment of electric mopeds has posed significant safety threats to elderly pedestrians due to their relatively higher speeds (compared to walking and biking) and increasing use and occupation of sidewalks. However, the country has few effective measures to prevent electric mopeds from injuring pedestrians, especially older people, who often have to shout at moped drivers to attract their attention and avoid collisions. Similarly, New York City is facing significant challenges due to the rapid growth of electric bicycle use. These challenges underscore the urgency of innovative solutions that prioritise mobility equity, promote age-sensitive planning, and establish regulatory frameworks that are attentive to older adults’ needs.

From the user perspective, older adults’ experiences with and acceptance of emerging transportation modes are influenced by multiple factors, including individual trust, perceived usefulness, perceived risks, user-friendly design, operational complexity and economic cost (Haghzare et al., 2021). Although emerging transportation modes are often touted as offering independence and convenience, older adults are generally cautious about adopting features such as autonomous driving technologies. This restraint stems from the perceived risk of technical failures, discrepancies between their driving habits and how the new vehicles operate, the loss of communication with drivers, and the need to master complex interfaces (Rahman et al., 2019). These attitudes reflect the mixed feelings of older adults about new transportation technologies and the disconnect between technological innovations and the needs of an ageing society. While technological advancements offer new possibilities for enhancing older adults’ mobility, addressing their psychological and physical barriers remains a critical challenge. Integrating multiscale planning and design approaches will ensure that older adults can truly benefit from transportation innovations.

# 2. Planning, design, and policy for older people’s mobility needs and healthy ageing

# 2.1. Key mobility concerns of older adults

Research by He and He (2025b) reveals that safety, attractiveness, convenience, continuity, and affordability are paramount for older adults. Safety is the highest priority because travelrelated accidents often have serious health consequences for older adults. Measures such as extended traffic light durations, pedestrian islands, smooth and obstacle-free pavements, pedestrian rights-of-way, and adequate nighttime lighting are critical for reducing accident risks and ensuring safe travel (Kim, 2019). New York City’s Department of Transportation has developed a system for ranking the safety of intersections based on historical traffic injury data. This system enables targeted implementation of safety measures in high-risk areas. Conversely, poorly designed pedestrian crossings and obstacles such as two-stage crossings, underpasses or overpasses can disrupt route continuity and reduce travel (Núñez et al., 2022). In collaboration with community organisations, the New York City Department of Transportation has also enriched streets with murals, dynamic lighting designs and sculptures, fostering cultural vibrancy and enhancing pedestrian appeal to increase walking rates among residents. Enhancements such as well-designed streetscapes with greenery, public restrooms and rest areas can improve the walking experience and encourage outdoor activity among older adults (Liu et al., 2024).

Accessibility is also vital. Urban environments must be designed to accommodate mobility aids such as wheelchairs, and public transport must offer barrier-free access to ensure inclusivity. Shanghai has retrofitted public buses for ageing populations: including introducing lowfloor or low-entry vehicles, constructing accessible bus stops, and providing seating and shelters at bus stations to enhance the travel experience for older passengers. Older adults on fixed incomes or those relying on public transport subsidies also need affordable transport options (Banister, 2019). Hong Kong’s $" \$ 2$ Fare Scheme” provides low-fare trips for residents aged 60 and above, enhancing transport affordability and accessibility.

Despite various urban initiatives to address the mobility needs of older adults, many are piecemeal and single-dimensional interventions that fail to achieve holistic and sustainable improvements (Loos et al., 2020). Meeting this group’s complex needs requires a systemic and integrated approach that ensures mutual support and coordination across different levels.

# 2.2. Multi-scale planning and integrated approaches

Transportation planning, design and policy development require a multi-scale integrated approach that addresses mobility needs at the personal, micro (structure or building), meso (neighborhood/community), and macro (city-wide/ regional) scales to create an inclusive and supportive transportation infrastructure and built environment to support older adults’ independence, mobility, and participation in out-of-home activities. On the personal scale, the focus should be on understanding and accommodating individual characteristics, capabilities and preferences that influence the travel patterns and transportation choices of older adults (Barbeau et al., 2010; Cui et al., 2017; O׳Hern & Oxley, 2015; Winters et al., 2015). At the micro scale, the planning and design of structures, especially in a context with increasing numbers of mixed-use and multi-level developments, should focus on ensuring seamless mobility within and between buildings (Carr et al., 2013; Hamraie, 2017). On the meso scale, the focus shifts to the connectivity of the built environment in neighbourhoods and communities to ensure access to amenities, activities and services that support active and healthy ageing (Levasseur et al., 2015; Rosso et al., 2011; Rosso et al., 2013). At the macro scale of city-wide or regional transportation planning and operations, age-friendly and universal design principles must be applied to transportation policy development, systemic improvements, and the setting of standards and guidelines (Fitzgerald & Caro, 2014; Steels, 2015). The interconnectedness of these scales necessitates cross-scale research and coordination to fully understand and address older adults’ mobility needs and develop a cohesive transportation network to better support independence and quality of life.

# 2.3. Global innovations and implementation challenges

Transportation planning, urban design and policy interventions must be coordinated and integrated into a multidimensional, systemic framework to address the complexity of older adults’ mobility needs (Metz, 2003). Globally, innovative initiatives are addressing the mobility challenges posed by ageing populations. Tokyo’s 2020 Universal Design initiative has improved transportation accessibility through the installation of elevators at subway stations, tactile paving for visually impaired individuals, priority seating in public transport, and staff training to foster a culture of respect and care for older adults (Huang et al., 2022). In Stockholm, urban planners have promoted active ageing through pedestrian-friendly streets and green spaces. The city’s Age-Friendly Streets project introduced wide, even sidewalks, accessible public toilets, strategically placed seating areas, and integrated parks and community gardens into residential areas to encourage outdoor activities and foster social interactions among older residents (Persson et al., 2018). Singapore’s Silver Zone initiative has created safe mobility areas for older adults through the extension of green-light time and the installation of gentle-gradient pedestrian pathways and S-shaped deceleration sections. These efforts have minimised traffic-related injuries among older adults and addressed their unique mobility and safety needs (Tan et al., 2024).

These initiatives represent significant progress in adopting integrated, multi-stakeholder approaches to address the mobility challenges of ageing societies. In each case, the solutions were tailored to the unique circumstances of a city and the needs of its older population. However, these projects still face challenges despite their initial success. They must ensure diverse and sustainable funding sources, foster coordination and information sharing across sectors and disciplines, and establish mechanisms for continuous evaluation and feedback on older adults’ dynamic needs. Integrated policy design, interdisciplinary research, and continuous attention and adaptation mechanisms are urgently needed to achieve their long-term sustainability and replicability.

# 3. Moving forward: an age-sensitive and interdisciplinary perspective

# 3.1. Key directions for age-sensitive transport policy

Providing accessible, safe and affordable transportation that citizens find satisfying is crucial for promoting independence, social engagement and quality of life among older adults (He et al., 2020). Incorporating an age-sensitive, interdisciplinary perspective into transport research and urban planning is no longer optional but imperative. Cities must prioritise identifying and meeting the needs and preferences of older adults to create inclusive, accessible, and equitable environments (Jehle et al., 2024). Emerging transport technologies must be developed and applied with older adults in mind. In the era of smart cities, ensuring equitable access to transport systems for older adults is essential. This involves designing userfriendly transportation apps and platforms; maintaining access to non-digital mobility services, such as paper timetables and phone-based bookings, for those unable or unwilling to use digital tools; and offering digital literacy training to bridge the technology gap (Wang et al., 2021).

# 3.2. Embracing interdisciplinary Collaboration

Effective planning for ageing societies extends beyond transportation, requiring interdisciplinary collaboration among transport researchers, urban planners, public health professionals, social workers, psychologists, gerontologists and architects. Collaboration with healthcare providers ensures that transport systems address the needs of individuals with limited mobility or chronic conditions (Rogers et al., 2024). Collaborations with community workers can provide assisted mobility solutions and promote health-enhancing activities to combat social isolation (Cirella et al., 2019). Urban and architectural designs grounded in inclusive design principles can integrate transportation systems with multifunctional public spaces, creating environments that address mobility barriers and support age-friendly urban living.

# 4. Conclusion

Healthy ageing is not only a social challenge but also an opportunity to drive innovation in transportation disciplines. Future transportation planning should adopt systemic and multiscale perspectives, promoting the integration of smart technologies and age-friendly designs. Strengthening interdisciplinary collaboration and centreing older adults’ needs in planning efforts will pave the way for exploring inclusive, lifecycle-oriented transportation models. The development and implementation of age-friendly transportation systems will lay the foundation for building inclusive, equitable and sustainable urban mobility frameworks for ageing societies.
