Barriers to Women’s Mobility in Metro Transit: A Comprehensive Literature Review
Abstract
Women globally encounter significant and multifaceted barriers to mobility within metro transit systems, impacting their access to education, employment, healthcare, and social participation. This literature review synthesizes recent academic contributions to identify, categorize, and critically analyze these impediments. Drawing from diverse geographical contexts, including South Asia, the Middle East, Europe, and Latin America, the paper reveals that barriers extend beyond mere infrastructural deficiencies to encompass pervasive safety and security threats, deeply entrenched socio-cultural and patriarchal norms, inadequate service quality, and systemic policy and planning shortcomings. Gender-based violence and harassment emerge as primary concerns, fostering fear and anxiety that compel women to adopt restrictive travel behaviors. Furthermore, the review highlights how women’s unique travel patterns, often characterized by multi-purpose and care-oriented trips, are frequently underserved by existing transport systems. Methodological approaches employed in the literature, predominantly qualitative and mixed-methods, underscore the subjective and affective dimensions of women’s mobility experiences. The paper concludes by identifying critical research gaps, particularly concerning the need for more robust gender-disaggregated data, intersectional analyses, and comprehensive evaluations of gender-responsive policy interventions, advocating for a holistic and rights-based approach to foster truly inclusive and equitable urban mobility.
Keywords
Women’s mobility, metro transit, public transport, gender barriers, safety, harassment, patriarchal norms, transport policy, urban planning, gender equity.
Introduction
Mobility is a fundamental human right, intrinsically linked to an individual’s ability to access opportunities, participate in society, and achieve personal well-being. For women, however, the experience of mobility, particularly within urban public transport systems, is often fraught with unique and profound challenges that significantly restrict their freedom and agency. These restrictions are not merely inconveniences but represent systemic barriers that impede women’s access to education, employment, healthcare, and civic life, thereby perpetuating gender inequality and undermining broader societal development goals (Hortelano et al., 2021; King et al., 2021; Singh & Choudhury, 2025). The concept of “metro transit” in this context encompasses a range of public transportation modes within metropolitan areas, including buses, trains, subways, and other mass transit options, which are crucial for daily commutes in densely populated urban environments. Understanding the specific barriers women face in utilizing these systems is paramount for developing truly inclusive, equitable, and sustainable urban spaces.
The existing literature consistently demonstrates that women’s mobility patterns and experiences differ significantly from those of men, influenced by a complex interplay of socio-cultural, economic, infrastructural, and psychological factors (Hortelano et al., 2021; Pourhashem et al., 2022; VUJIČIĆ et al., 2025). While transport systems are often designed under a gender-neutral assumption, the reality is that they are deeply gendered, reflecting and reinforcing existing societal inequalities (Thaher, 2024). This review aims to synthesize the current academic understanding of the barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit, drawing upon a diverse body of research from various global contexts. By critically examining the findings, methodologies, and policy implications presented in the retrieved literature, this paper seeks to provide a comprehensive overview of the challenges, identify common themes and regional specificities, and highlight crucial areas for future research and intervention. The subsequent sections will delve into the pervasive threats to safety and security, the insidious role of socio-cultural norms, the tangible deficiencies in infrastructure and service quality, the economic and psychological costs borne by women, and the critical shortcomings in policy and planning that collectively hinder women’s equitable access to urban mobility.
The Pervasive Threat of Safety and Security Concerns
One of the most frequently cited and profoundly impactful barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit is the pervasive threat to their safety and security, encompassing gender-based violence (GBV), harassment, and crime. This issue is not confined to specific regions but is a global phenomenon, albeit with varying manifestations and degrees of severity, profoundly shaping women’s travel choices and experiences (Ceccato et al., 2024a; Infante-Vargas & Boyer, 2021; Kacharo et al., 2022; King et al., 2021). The literature consistently highlights that the fear and actual experience of violence significantly deter women from using public transport, forcing them to adopt coping mechanisms that often restrict their mobility or impose additional costs.
In South and Southeast Asian contexts, the problem of sexual harassment and violence on public transport is particularly acute, forming a “toxic base” that restricts women’s mobility and leads to victim-blaming if incidents are reported Sil et al. (2023). Studies from Bangladesh, for instance, reveal that women face multiple threats, including GBV, harassment, crime, and even traffic-related injuries, leading to high levels of anxiety and fear during commutes King et al. (2021). Despite these dangers, women are often “captive travelers,” compelled to prioritize affordability and availability over safety due to limited alternatives King et al. (2021). Similarly, in Indian cities like Delhi and Kolkata, sexual harassment remains a prevalent challenge, underscoring the urgent need for gender-responsive transport policies Joshi et al. (2022). Research in Bangladesh further indicates that while harassment is widespread across genders, there are considerable gender differences in the perception of mobility hindrances and desirable safety precautions, with women being disproportionately affected Yasir et al. (2022). Interestingly, this study also suggests that people in smaller towns might be more likely to experience harassment than those in larger cities, possibly due to underreporting in less urbanized areas Yasir et al. (2022). The findings from Hyderabad, India, corroborate these concerns, emphasizing that buses are common locations for physical harassment, particularly touching, and overcrowding is a major concern for women commuters Ali et al. (2025). These studies collectively paint a grim picture where the physical environment of public transport, especially when overcrowded, becomes a site of vulnerability for women.
Beyond South Asia, similar patterns of violence and insecurity are documented. In Saltillo, Mexico, gender-based violence against women using public transport is an everyday reality, leading to re-victimization and significant psychological effects that extend beyond the immediate event, including financial and emotional repercussions and severe limitations on mobility Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021). The study in Hawassa city, Ethiopia, found that over half of women and girls experienced more than one type of violence (physical, sexual, psychological) while using public transport, with factors like age, marital status, type of transport, travel time, and management of services significantly contributing to these incidents Kacharo et al. (2022). These findings underscore that the problem is systemic and deeply embedded in the public transport experience for women in many developing countries.
Even in countries with strong reputations for gender equality, such as Sweden, women continue to fear traveling by public transport, especially in the evenings, indicating the persistence of patriarchal norms that limit their use and access to public spaces Lindkvist (2024). Research on railway stations in Sweden reveals that women, particularly those identifying as LGBTQI+/Non-binary/Other, are more likely to feel less safe, with factors like lack of staff and poor maintenance contributing to lower safety perceptions Ceccato et al. (2024b). Furthermore, a study focusing on rural-urban contexts in Sweden highlights that while rural women might feel safer than their urban counterparts, they are more willing to take precautionary measures, such as traveling with someone in the evening or avoiding certain stations, demonstrating that the fear of victimization influences behavior regardless of the perceived safety level Ceccato et al. (2024a). These European examples demonstrate that while the overt forms of violence might differ from those in developing countries, the underlying fear and its impact on women’s mobility are universal.
The psychological impact of these safety concerns is profound. Women often internalize expectations of violence and exclusionary cues, which manifest as “fear-based barriers” to public transport use Kerzhner et al. (2018). This fear compels women to modify their travel patterns, minimize travel, or even avoid certain destinations altogether, thereby limiting their social and economic participation King et al. (2021); Kerzhner et al. (2018). The “disruptive affects” of unsafe conditions produce emotional ordeals, leading to a sense of despair that forces women to adjust, accept, or opt out of public transport Roy et al. (2024). This emotional toll is a critical, yet often overlooked, dimension of mobility barriers, emphasizing the need for feminist inquiries into gendered mobility inequalities that consider the role of affects and emotions Roy et al. (2024). The cumulative effect of these safety and security threats is a significant reduction in women’s freedom of movement, undermining their fundamental right to equal access to urban resources and opportunities.
Socio-Cultural and Patriarchal Norms as Underlying Impediments
Beyond the immediate threats to physical safety, deeply entrenched socio-cultural and patriarchal norms constitute a fundamental and often invisible barrier to women’s mobility in metro transit. These norms dictate women’s roles, behaviors, and acceptable presence in public spaces, profoundly influencing their travel patterns, mode choices, and experiences. The literature consistently demonstrates that these societal expectations, rather than being mere background factors, actively shape the design and experience of transport systems, particularly in conservative or traditionally patriarchal societies.
In South and Southeast Asia, patriarchal societal norms are identified as a core component of the unique mobility barriers women encounter, forming a “toxic base” that exacerbates issues like sexual harassment and victim-blaming Sil et al. (2023). These norms contribute to a hostile social climate that, when combined with poor services, severely restricts women’s mobility Sil et al. (2023). For instance, in Pakistan, conservative norms are explicitly linked to greater restrictions on women’s physical mobility, which in turn limits their labor market opportunities Field & Vyborny (2022). The study in Lahore, Pakistan, experimentally varied access to transport services and found that women’s increased job searching was primarily driven by a women-only transport option, suggesting that “safety and social acceptability, rather than simply cost, are key constraints” Field & Vyborny (2022). This highlights how the need to conform to societal expectations of propriety and safety, often enforced by patriarchal norms, can override practical considerations like cost.
Similarly, in the Dammam Metropolitan Region of Saudi Arabia, women’s mobility has been historically limited and challenged due to socio-cultural barriers, alongside spatio-temporal constraints Al-Rashid et al. (2020). The absence of gender-responsive design and infrastructure in public buses, coupled with societal expectations, forces women to rely on taxis, where concerns about privacy and insecurity persist, especially when traveling alone or with children Al-Rashid et al. (2020). This illustrates how socio-cultural norms directly influence transport choices, often pushing women towards less sustainable or more expensive private options in pursuit of perceived safety and social acceptability. The broader context of promoting women’s well-being through socially sustainable urban transport in developing countries, such as Amman, Jordan, also underscores the need to address cultural biases in planning processes and integrate women’s voices to overcome these deeply rooted barriers Thaher (2024).
The impact of these norms extends to the psychological realm. In Jerusalem, Palestinian women internalize expectations of violence and exclusionary linguistic and visual cues, which act as fear-based barriers to public transport use Kerzhner et al. (2018). This internalization reflects a societal environment where women are conditioned to anticipate threats, leading them to minimize travel or avoid certain areas Kerzhner et al. (2018). The “sense of despair” that emerges from negotiating overcrowded, unsafe, and unreliable public transport conditions, as observed in Kolkata, is also deeply intertwined with the societal context that permits such conditions to persist and disproportionately affect women Roy et al. (2024).
Even in societies that outwardly champion gender equality, such as Sweden, patriarchal norms continue to exert influence. Despite policy recommendations and legislation aimed at counteracting discrimination, women still express fear when using public transport and being out in the evenings Lindkvist (2024). This suggests that the “mythical mantra” of gender equality does not fully eradicate the underlying societal norms that delimit women’s use and access to public space and public transport, demonstrating the insidious and persistent nature of these barriers even in progressive contexts Lindkvist (2024). The study emphasizes that women’s travel choices are affected by concerns about safety and the necessary adaptations to perceived threatening situations, which are ultimately rooted in societal expectations and gendered perceptions of public space Lindkvist (2024).
The cumulative effect of these socio-cultural and patriarchal norms is a significant constraint on women’s autonomy and freedom of movement. They not only contribute to direct safety threats but also subtly shape women’s perceptions of their own mobility, their acceptable presence in public, and the choices they feel empowered to make. Addressing these deeply ingrained societal structures is therefore crucial for any meaningful progress towards gender-equitable urban mobility, requiring interventions that go beyond mere infrastructural improvements to challenge and transform underlying cultural biases and expectations.
Deficiencies in Public Transport Infrastructure and Service Quality
Beyond the socio-cultural and safety dimensions, tangible deficiencies in public transport infrastructure and service quality represent significant practical barriers to women’s mobility. These issues, ranging from inadequate routes and unreliable services to poor vehicle design and maintenance, directly impact women’s ability to access and comfortably use metro transit, often exacerbating other existing challenges. The literature consistently points to these operational and physical shortcomings as critical impediments, particularly in developing countries, but also in more developed contexts.
In many regions, the fundamental lack of adequate public transportation options is a primary concern. In the Dammam Metropolitan Region of Saudi Arabia, for example, the limited availability of public transportation leads to an increased demand for private vehicles, which contradicts global sustainability goals Al-Rashid et al. (2020). The existing public transport service is characterized by limited operational routes, hours, and infrastructure, forcing women to predominantly prefer taxis for shopping and leisure activities Al-Rashid et al. (2020). This highlights how insufficient public transport provision directly restricts women’s choices and often pushes them towards more expensive or less sustainable alternatives. Similarly, in Bangladesh, despite a high population density, the country lacks a well-coordinated mass transit system, leading most people, especially vulnerable groups like women, to rely on public transportation that presents enormous challenges Yasir et al. (2022).
The quality and reliability of existing services are also major issues. In Kolkata, India, women frequently encounter overcrowded, unsafe, and unreliable conditions, which are identified as major barriers to accessing public transport mobility Roy et al. (2024). These “disruptive affects” of poor service quality produce emotional ordeals, increase travel time and costs, and ultimately restrict mobility, compelling women to adjust, accept, or opt out of public transport Roy et al. (2024). The unreliability of services can disrupt women’s complex daily schedules, which often involve multiple stops for childcare, household chores, and work, making punctuality and predictability crucial. In Jerusalem, Palestinian women face multifaceted barriers including poor infrastructure and low levels of public transportation, alongside ethnic segregation and a hostile social climate Kerzhner et al. (2018). These combined factors create significant limitations on public transport use and destination choice, demonstrating how infrastructural shortcomings intersect with other barriers to create a highly exclusionary urban environment Kerzhner et al. (2018).
Specific infrastructural and design flaws within vehicles and at transit stops also contribute to women’s insecurity and discomfort. The study in Saudi Arabia points out the inadequacy of supporting infrastructures and interior design of public buses to women’s needs, noting the absence of gender-responsive design Al-Rashid et al. (2020). This lack of consideration for women’s specific requirements, such as privacy or space for children, forces them into less desirable travel options. In Hyderabad, India, infrastructural characteristics like the presence of CCTV and lighting at transit stops and within public transport vehicles are identified as the most important attributes influencing women’s safety and security perceptions Ali et al. (2025). The study also highlights bus overcrowding as a main concern, directly linking physical conditions to experiences of harassment Ali et al. (2025). This suggests that basic design elements, when neglected, can significantly compromise women’s sense of safety.
Even in European contexts, issues related to infrastructure and maintenance are relevant. Research on railway stations in Sweden indicates that a lack of staff and poor maintenance are significantly associated with lower levels of safety perceived by passengers, including women Ceccato et al. (2024b). These findings underscore that the physical environment of transit spaces, including its upkeep and human presence, plays a crucial role in shaping safety perceptions and experiences. The cumulative effect of these deficiencies in infrastructure and service quality is a public transport system that is not only inconvenient but often actively hostile to women’s needs, forcing them to make difficult choices that compromise their safety, time, and financial resources. Addressing these issues requires a conscious effort to integrate gender-sensitive design principles and service provision into transport planning, moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach to acknowledge and cater to the diverse needs of women commuters.
Economic, Temporal, and Psychological Costs of Restricted Mobility
The barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit are not merely abstract concepts; they translate into tangible economic, temporal, and psychological costs that disproportionately burden women and impede their overall well-being and societal participation. These costs are often interconnected, with one type of burden exacerbating another, creating a complex web of disadvantages.
Economically, restricted mobility directly impacts women’s labor market opportunities and financial independence. In cities with conservative norms or high crime rates, women face greater restrictions on their physical mobility, which limits their access to jobs and reduces the pool of workers firms can attract Field & Vyborny (2022). The experimental evidence from Pakistan, showing that women’s job searching significantly increases with access to women-only transport, underscores that safety and social acceptability are critical constraints, often outweighing mere cost considerations Field & Vyborny (2022). When public transport is unsafe or unreliable, women are often forced to use more expensive private modes, such as taxis, as observed in Saudi Arabia, where poor public transport service drives a predominant preference for taxis for shopping and leisure Al-Rashid et al. (2020). This shift to private transport incurs higher costs, reducing disposable income and further limiting economic agency. The financial repercussions of gender-based violence, extending beyond the event itself, also contribute to these economic burdens Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021).
Temporally, women often experience increased travel times and greater inconvenience due to inadequate transport systems. Women’s travel patterns are typically more complex than men’s, characterized by “trip chaining” – combining multiple purposes such as work, childcare, and household errands – and often occurring outside traditional peak hours VUJIČIĆ et al. (2025); Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken (2020); Hortelano et al. (2021). In Novi Sad, Serbia, research indicates that women’s trips are more related to daily duties and are scattered even outside peak hours, unlike men who often have more precise peak hours and higher usage of private vehicles VUJIČIĆ et al. (2025). These complex, care-oriented trips are frequently underserved by transport systems designed primarily for male-dominated, work-related commutes, leading to longer travel times and more arduous journeys Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken (2020). The “disruptive affects” of overcrowded, unsafe, and unreliable public transport conditions, as seen in Kolkata, directly increase travel time and costs, further restricting mobility Roy et al. (2024). This additional time burden can limit women’s capacity for paid work, education, or leisure, perpetuating a cycle of disadvantage.
Psychologically, the constant threat of violence and the experience of navigating inadequate systems impose a significant emotional toll. Women using public transport in Bangladesh report high levels of anxiety and fear due to multiple threats to their safety King et al. (2021). In Saltillo, Mexico, women experience not only physical violence but also re-victimization and profound psychological effects from violent episodes Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021). The “sense of despair” that emerges from negotiating challenging public transport conditions compels women to adjust, accept, or even opt out of public transport, indicating a deep emotional burden Roy et al. (2024). This constant vigilance and the need to adapt behaviors to mitigate risks, such as changing travel patterns or restricting travel frequency, contribute to chronic stress and reduce overall quality of life King et al. (2021); Lindkvist (2024). The internalization of expectations of violence and exclusionary cues, as observed among Palestinian women in Jerusalem, serves as a “fear-based barrier” that limits public transport use and destination choice, highlighting the profound psychological impact of a hostile urban environment Kerzhner et al. (2018).
Furthermore, the concept of the “value of travel time” (VOTT) also reveals gender differences, with women often having a higher perception of the worthwhileness of travel time for walking compared to men using motorized modes Pourhashem et al. (2022). This suggests that women may derive different subjective values from their travel experiences, with perceived mood and enjoyable travel time influencing choices like ridesharing or private motorized modes as passengers Pourhashem et al. (2022). The cumulative effect of these economic, temporal, and psychological costs is a significant erosion of women’s autonomy and well-being. It underscores that addressing mobility barriers requires not only physical improvements but also a deep understanding of the subjective experiences and the multifaceted burdens women bear, advocating for transport systems that prioritize their safety, convenience, and emotional comfort.
Policy, Planning, and Design Shortcomings
The persistence of barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit is often rooted in systemic shortcomings in transport policy, planning, and design. Historically, transport planning has been largely gender-neutral or male-centric, failing to acknowledge and address the distinct mobility needs, patterns, and safety concerns of women. This oversight results in discriminatory policies and infrastructure that perpetuate inequalities, as highlighted across various global contexts.
A fundamental issue is the lack of adequate inclusive policies and protection laws from authorities, particularly in South and Southeast Asian countries Sil et al. (2023). Despite international guidelines, women’s mobility remains restricted and unsafe due to this policy vacuum Sil et al. (2023). The absence of gender-responsive transport policies means that the unique challenges women face, such as sexual harassment, are not systematically addressed Joshi et al. (2022). Research from Indian cities like Delhi and Kolkata emphasizes the critical need for framing interventions in a rights-based manner, institutionalizing gender-disaggregated data to inform policies, and sensitizing institutions, including law enforcement, on gender equity and women’s rights to the city Joshi et al. (2022). This points to a broader institutional failure to integrate a gender perspective into transport governance.
Discriminatory planning policies are also evident in specific urban environments. In Jerusalem, for instance, Palestinian women face limitations due to discriminatory planning policies, alongside poor infrastructure and low service levels Kerzhner et al. (2018). This suggests that planning decisions can actively contribute to exclusion, rather than merely being passively inadequate. Similarly, in Saudi Arabia, the absence of a gender-responsive design and infrastructure in public buses forces women to use less ideal alternatives, demonstrating how design choices, or the lack thereof, can be inherently discriminatory Al-Rashid et al. (2020). The study in Amman, Jordan, further underscores the non-neutral nature of transportation systems and the imperative for gender-conscious assessments within urban transport planning, advocating for a departure from one-size-fits-all approaches Thaher (2024).
The concept of “fairness” in transport is crucial here, as women generally experience disproportionate fairness issues when using transport Hail & McQuaid (2021). Different concepts of fairness—equality of opportunity, equity, and justice (procedural, social, distributional)—all have distinct implications for women. Factors such as safety, cost, physical design, and responsibilities like childcare highlight how existing systems often fail to meet vertical fairness (more disadvantaged people receiving greater support) and horizontal fairness (similar people being treated similarly) principles for women Hail & McQuaid (2021). This calls for further research into frameworks for a more comprehensive inclusion and balance of these fairness concepts in both transport policy and practice Hail & McQuaid (2021).
Proposed policy interventions often include “women-only solutions,” such as women-only transport services. Experimental evidence from Pakistan shows that a women-only transport treatment arm significantly impacts women’s job searching, suggesting its effectiveness in addressing safety and social acceptability concerns Field & Vyborny (2022). Similarly, studies in Bangladesh recommend female-only rides, especially at night, as a beneficial safety precaution Yasir et al. (2022). While such solutions can offer immediate relief and a sense of security, they also raise questions about segregation and whether they address the root causes of insecurity in public spaces. Other interventions include leveraging technology and improving infrastructural design, such as better lighting and CCTV, to enhance safety Joshi et al. (2022); Ali et al. (2025).
In Europe, despite policy initiatives and regulations addressing gender equality in transport, challenges persist. A review of European transport research and innovation highlights the need for planners and policymakers to consider all impacts on gender equality to achieve sustainable and inclusive transport Hortelano et al. (2021). The study identifies critical issues in the European transport sector and provides policy recommendations to overcome major barriers to gender equality, emphasizing the need for more women in transport research and innovation to ensure a gender-sensitive perspective in decision-making Hortelano et al. (2021).
Recent research also calls for innovative planning tools and approaches. The concept of a “Pink Map” is proposed as a data-driven urban planning tool to map safe and accessible urban spaces for women and the elderly, leveraging GIS mapping, community participation, and real-time feedback Singh & Choudhury (2025). This highlights the need for technology-enabled safety solutions and gender- and age-sensitive urban transport policies. Furthermore, the importance of “gender-age-sensitive mobility policies” is emphasized, taking into account the intersectionality of transit safety and reflecting women’s and girls’ safety needs, particularly in diverse rural and urban contexts Ceccato et al. (2024a).
In summary, the shortcomings in policy, planning, and design are not merely passive omissions but active contributors to the barriers women face. Addressing these requires a paradigm shift towards consciously inclusive route and infrastructure planning, immediate changes to symbology and behavior of transport personnel, and long-term commitments to gender-responsive policies that are informed by disaggregated data and a deep understanding of women’s diverse needs and experiences Kerzhner et al. (2018); Joshi et al. (2022); Thaher (2024).
Methodological Approaches and Research Gaps
The body of literature on barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit employs a variety of methodological approaches, predominantly qualitative and mixed-methods, to capture the complex and often subjective nature of women’s experiences. While these methods have yielded rich insights, they also present certain limitations and highlight significant research gaps that warrant future attention.
Common methodological approaches include in-depth interviews, focus group discussions, and surveys. For instance, Kerzhner et al. (2018) utilized eight in-depth interviews and 38 participants in a focus group of Palestinian women to examine their public transport and private car use in Jerusalem, shedding light on individual needs and decisions in an exclusionary urban environment. Similarly, Roy et al. (2024) employed in-depth interviews and visual surveys to delve into the affective realm of women’s commutes in Kolkata, comprehending how emotions shape accessibility, acceptability, and affordability. In-depth interviews with women participants in Delhi and Kolkata were also central to Joshi et al. (2022)’s study on devising gender-responsive transport policies in South Asia. Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021) used a mixed-method approach, combining a survey (N=611) and interviews (N=11) with transit users and an activist in Saltillo, Mexico, to detail the pervasiveness of gender-based violence. A survey campaign involving structured interviews and online questionnaires was launched in the Dammam Metropolitan Region, Saudi Arabia, to understand gender experiences of public transport users Al-Rashid et al. (2020). Qualitative interviews and data from the Swedish National Statistics were used by Lindkvist (2024) to explore mobility practices and strategies in Sweden. King et al. (2021) analyzed qualitative and quantitative data from 200 women in Bangladesh to examine travel patterns and experiences, while Yasir et al. (2022) conducted a detailed questionnaire survey across three cities in Bangladesh to investigate harassment and challenges faced by public transport users. Ali et al. (2025) employed a mixed-method approach, including a survey of 583 women commuters in Hyderabad, India, and econometric models (binary logit and ordered probit) to analyze factors influencing safety and security perceptions.
Quantitative methods, such as regression models, are also utilized to analyze travel behavior and perceptions. Pourhashem et al. (2022), for example, used a multinomial regression model based on data from 1406 female and 1486 male travelers across eight European countries to scrutinize gender differences in the perception of the value of travel time and mode choice behavior. Kacharo et al. (2022) employed binary logistic regression for quantitative data analysis from 199 respondents in Hawassa city, Ethiopia, to assess women and girls’ safety and security in public transport. Ceccato et al. (2024b) and Ceccato et al. (2024a) utilized exploratory data analysis, logistic regression models, and chi-square analysis to assess patterns of victimization and safety perceptions among railway passengers in Sweden, including rural-urban contexts. The Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) was employed by Thaher (2024), surveying 38 stakeholders and decision-makers in Amman, Jordan, to investigate the intersection of sustainable urban transport planning, gender perspectives, and women’s well-being. VUJIČIĆ et al. (2025) analyzed mobility, modal split, and distribution by travel reason as a function of gender using travel diaries collected by household surveys in Novi Sad, Serbia.
Despite these varied approaches, the literature identifies several limitations in common methods adopted to measure women’s mobility barriers Sil et al. (2023). One significant gap is the often-insufficient focus on the “affective realm” – how emotions and feelings shape accessibility, acceptability, and affordability of public transport for women Roy et al. (2024). This suggests a need for more nuanced qualitative methodologies that can capture the emotional and psychological dimensions of travel experiences.
Several key research gaps emerge from this review: (1) Lack of Gender-Disaggregated Data and Institutionalization: Many studies highlight the absence of systematically collected gender-disaggregated data to inform interventions and policies Joshi et al. (2022); Thaher (2024). Without robust data, it is challenging to accurately assess the extent of gendered mobility inequalities and the effectiveness of interventions. (2) Limited Intersectional Analysis: While some studies acknowledge the varying experiences of different groups of women (e.g., young women, LGBTQI+ women, women with childcare responsibilities) Ceccato et al. (2024b); Ceccato et al. (2024a); Singh & Choudhury (2025), a more comprehensive intersectional approach is needed to understand how gender intersects with age, socioeconomic status, ethnicity, disability, and other factors to create unique mobility challenges. (3) Evaluation of Policy Effectiveness: While many papers propose policy recommendations, there is a need for more rigorous evaluations of the actual effectiveness of implemented gender-responsive transport policies and interventions in diverse contexts Sil et al. (2023); Hortelano et al. (2021). This includes assessing the long-term impacts of “women-only” solutions and other safety measures. (4) Understanding Psychological and Emotional Impacts: While some studies touch upon fear and anxiety, a deeper, more systematic exploration of the psychological and emotional burdens of unsafe and unreliable transport is needed, moving beyond mere reporting of incidents to understanding the chronic stress and coping mechanisms Roy et al. (2024); Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021). (5) Integration of Fairness Principles: There is a call for further research into setting frameworks for a more comprehensive inclusion of, and balance between, different concepts of fairness and their interactions in both transport policy and practice Hail & McQuaid (2021). (6) Impact of Emerging Technologies: The potential future impacts of autonomous vehicles on in-vehicle security and gender equity are explored by Yasar & Basaran (2025), indicating a nascent but critical area for future research as transport technologies evolve. (7) Rural-Urban Contexts: While much research focuses on urban areas, Ceccato et al. (2024a) highlights the need for gender-age-sensitive mobility policies that consider the specific safety needs of women and girls in rural contexts, suggesting a gap in understanding mobility challenges beyond dense metropolitan centers.
Addressing these methodological and research gaps will enable a more nuanced, evidence-based, and ultimately more effective approach to creating truly inclusive and equitable metro transit systems for women worldwide.
Conclusion
The comprehensive review of the literature unequivocally demonstrates that women’s mobility in metro transit is hindered by a complex and interconnected web of barriers that are deeply rooted in societal structures, infrastructural deficiencies, and policy shortcomings. These impediments are not isolated incidents but rather systemic challenges that manifest globally, albeit with regional specificities and varying degrees of severity. The synthesis of findings from diverse geographical contexts—including South Asia, the Middle East, Europe, and Latin America—reveals a consistent pattern of disadvantage and restriction for women navigating urban public transport.
Foremost among these barriers is the pervasive threat to safety and security, encompassing gender-based violence, sexual harassment, and crime. Studies from Bangladesh King et al. (2021); Yasir et al. (2022), India Joshi et al. (2022); Ali et al. (2025), Mexico Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021), Ethiopia Kacharo et al. (2022), and even Sweden Lindkvist (2024); Ceccato et al. (2024b); Ceccato et al. (2024a) consistently highlight that the fear and actual experience of violence profoundly shape women’s travel behaviors, leading to restricted mobility, anxiety, and psychological distress. This fear is often exacerbated by socio-cultural and patriarchal norms that dictate women’s roles and acceptable presence in public spaces, contributing to a hostile social climate and victim-blaming Sil et al. (2023); Field & Vyborny (2022); Al-Rashid et al. (2020); Kerzhner et al. (2018). These norms often compel women to choose less sustainable or more expensive private transport options for perceived safety and social acceptability.
Furthermore, deficiencies in public transport infrastructure and service quality present tangible obstacles. Inadequate routes, limited operational hours, unreliable services, overcrowding, and poor vehicle design and maintenance are common complaints across studies from Jerusalem Kerzhner et al. (2018), Kolkata Roy et al. (2024), Saudi Arabia Al-Rashid et al. (2020), and Bangladesh King et al. (2021); Yasir et al. (2022). These shortcomings not only make travel inconvenient but also contribute to feelings of insecurity and increase the risk of harassment, particularly in overcrowded conditions Ali et al. (2025). The cumulative effect of these barriers translates into significant economic, temporal, and psychological costs for women. Restricted mobility limits labor market opportunities Field & Vyborny (2022), increases travel expenses Al-Rashid et al. (2020), and leads to longer, more arduous commutes due to women’s complex, multi-purpose travel patterns VUJIČIĆ et al. (2025); Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken (2020). The emotional toll, characterized by anxiety, fear, and despair, further diminishes women’s quality of life and participation in society Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021); Roy et al. (2024).
Underlying these challenges are critical policy, planning, and design shortcomings. Transport planning has historically been gender-neutral or male-centric, resulting in discriminatory policies and infrastructure that fail to address women’s distinct needs Sil et al. (2023); Kerzhner et al. (2018); Al-Rashid et al. (2020); Thaher (2024). The lack of gender-disaggregated data, insufficient institutional sensitization, and the absence of rights-based approaches in policy formulation are recurrent themes Joshi et al. (2022). While solutions like women-only transport Field & Vyborny (2022); Yasir et al. (2022) and technology-enabled safety measures Ali et al. (2025); Singh & Choudhury (2025) are proposed, a holistic approach that integrates fairness principles Hail & McQuaid (2021) and gender-age-sensitive planning Ceccato et al. (2024a) is essential.
Despite the valuable insights provided by the diverse methodological approaches, several research gaps remain. There is a pressing need for more robust, systematically collected gender-disaggregated data to accurately inform policy and evaluate interventions. Future research should also embrace a more comprehensive intersectional analysis, understanding how gender interacts with other social categories like age, socioeconomic status, and ethnicity to shape mobility experiences. Rigorous evaluations of the long-term effectiveness of gender-responsive policies and interventions are crucial, moving beyond mere implementation to assess actual impact. Furthermore, a deeper exploration of the psychological and emotional burdens, including chronic stress and coping mechanisms, is warranted. Finally, as urban environments and technologies evolve, research must also consider the implications of emerging transport modes, such as autonomous vehicles Yasar & Basaran (2025), and the specific challenges faced by women in diverse rural-urban contexts Ceccato et al. (2024a).
In conclusion, achieving truly inclusive and equitable urban mobility requires a paradigm shift in transport planning and policy. This necessitates a conscious, rights-based approach that prioritizes women’s safety, acknowledges their unique travel patterns, challenges underlying patriarchal norms, and actively involves women’s voices in the design and implementation of transport systems. By addressing these multifaceted barriers comprehensively, cities can move towards creating metro transit systems that empower women, enhance their well-being, and foster more just and sustainable urban environments for all.

The Pervasive Threat of Safety and Security Concerns
One of the most significant and frequently cited barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit is the pervasive threat to their safety and security. This threat manifests in various forms, ranging from overt gender-based violence and harassment to a generalized sense of fear and insecurity that compels women to adopt restrictive travel behaviors. The literature consistently highlights that these concerns are not isolated incidents but rather an everyday reality for many women, profoundly impacting their access to public spaces and services.
Gender-Based Violence and Harassment: A Global Epidemic
Gender-based violence (GBV) and sexual harassment are alarmingly common experiences for women using public transport worldwide, acting as a direct and severe impediment to their mobility. Studies from diverse regions underscore the universality of this problem. For instance, in South and Southeast Asia, women frequently encounter severe forms of sexual harassment when riding public transport, often compounded by victim-blaming if incidents are reported, as highlighted by Sil et al. (2023). This toxic environment, rooted in patriarchal societal norms, creates an unsafe travel experience that restricts women’s freedom of movement. Similarly, in Bangladesh, women using paid transport for work or education face multiple threats to their safety, including GBV, harassment, and crime, leading to high levels of anxiety and fear, as documented by King et al. (2021). Despite these dangers, women are often “captive travelers,” forced to prioritize price and availability over safety, further exacerbating their vulnerability.
The experiences in Latin America echo these findings. In Saltillo, Mexico, GBV is an everyday reality for women using public transport, with pervasive effects extending beyond the immediate event to include financial and emotional repercussions and significant limitations on mobility, as detailed by Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021). This study, utilizing a mixed-method approach, revealed the widespread nature of violence and the issue of re-victimization, underscoring the urgent need for policy change. In Ethiopia, a study in Hawassa city found that over half of women and girls experienced more than one type of violence—physical, sexual, or psychological—while using public transport. Kacharo et al. (2022) identified factors such as age, marital status, type of public transport, travel time, and facility management as significant contributors to these violent incidents, emphasizing the serious threat to women’s safety and security in accessing urban mobility. Even in countries with strong reputations for gender equality, like Sweden, women still fear traveling by public transport and being out in the evenings, indicating that patriarchal norms continue to allow for such threats, as Lindkvist (2024) points out. This suggests that while the overt forms and frequency might vary, the underlying issue of women’s vulnerability in public spaces is a global challenge.
The consequences of these experiences are profound. Roy et al. (2024), in their study on Kolkata, delves into the “affective realm” of women’s commutes, demonstrating how the disruptive affects of overcrowded, unsafe, and unreliable conditions produce emotional ordeals. These emotional burdens, stemming from the constant threat of harassment and violence, compel women to adjust, accept, or even opt out of public transport, thereby restricting their mobility and impacting their accessibility, acceptability, and affordability of transport. The entanglement of affects, emotions, reactions, and consequences highlights the deep psychological toll of unsafe transit environments. Furthermore, Yasar & Basaran (2025) emphasizes that despite existing security measures, women experience criminal and violent acts more frequently in public transit, leading to behavioral adaptations that ultimately undermine transportation equity by creating barriers to essential services.
Fear and Perceived Safety: The Invisible Walls
Beyond direct experiences of violence, the perception of unsafety and the omnipresent fear of potential harm act as powerful “invisible barriers” to women’s mobility. This fear is often disproportionate to actual victimization rates but is nonetheless a significant determinant of travel behavior. Kerzhner et al. (2018), in their study of Palestinian women in Jerusalem, vividly describes how women internalize expectations of violence and exclusionary cues, which serve as fear-based barriers to public transport use. This psychological burden, alongside physical barriers, significantly limits their public transport use and destination choices. The study reveals that many women respond by avoiding certain areas, minimizing travel, or opting for private cars, even if less practical, to feel safer.
The concept of perceived safety is further explored by Ceccato et al. (2024b), who investigated safety perceptions among railway passengers in Sweden. Their findings indicate that passengers’ safety perceptions are more influenced by the physical and social characteristics of transit environments—such as lack of staff and poor maintenance—than by actual victimization rates. Crucially, women, and particularly those identifying as LGBTQI+/Non-binary/Other, reported a higher likelihood of feeling less safe. This highlights the intersectional nature of perceived safety, where multiple identities can amplify vulnerability. Similarly, Ali et al. (2025), examining women commuters in Hyderabad, India, found that infrastructural characteristics like the presence of CCTV and lighting at transit stops and in vehicles were paramount in influencing women’s safety and security perceptions. Age, family opinion, police presence, and traveling in groups, especially at night, also significantly shaped these perceptions, underscoring the multifaceted nature of perceived safety.
The distinction between actual victimization and perceived safety is critical for policy formulation. While reducing actual crime is paramount, addressing the fear of crime requires a broader approach that includes improving environmental design, increasing visible staffing, and fostering a sense of community ownership over public spaces. Ceccato et al. (2024a) further elaborates on this, noting that while rural women in Sweden might feel safer than their urban counterparts, they are often more willing to take precautionary measures, such as traveling with someone in the evening or avoiding certain stations. This demonstrates that even in contexts where direct victimization might be lower, the need for vigilance and adaptation persists, indicating that the psychological burden of potential threat is widespread.
Behavioral Adaptations: Restricting Freedom
In response to these pervasive safety and security threats, women frequently adopt a range of behavioral adaptations that, while aimed at mitigating risk, ultimately restrict their mobility and autonomy. These adaptations include altering travel patterns, avoiding certain routes or times, and even foregoing public transport altogether. King et al. (2021) found that women in Bangladesh, unable to choose safe transport options, attempted to mitigate risks by changing their travel patterns and behavior, and by restricting their travel frequency. This often means limiting social activities, educational pursuits, or employment opportunities that require travel during perceived unsafe hours or to unsafe locations.
Yasar & Basaran (2025) explicitly states that women’s experiences of criminal and violent acts in public transit lead to behavioral adaptations such as trip avoidance, mode shifts, or destination changes. These adjustments create tangible barriers to accessing jobs, education, healthcare, and social services, thereby undermining the fundamental principle of transportation equity. The study by Kerzhner et al. (2018) on Palestinian women in Jerusalem provides concrete examples of these adaptations, where women actively avoid West Jerusalem and minimize travel, or adopt private cars as a safer, albeit sometimes less practical, alternative. This demonstrates a forced choice, where the desire for safety overrides other considerations like cost or convenience.
The emotional toll of these adaptations is also significant. Roy et al. (2024) illustrates how the “sense of despair” emerging from unsafe conditions compels women to adjust, accept, and even opt out of public transport. This constant negotiation with fear and risk transforms everyday commutes into emotional ordeals, impacting women’s mental well-being and further limiting their participation in public life. The need to constantly be alert and take precautionary measures, as observed by Ceccato et al. (2024a), even in seemingly safer rural contexts, underscores the persistent burden placed on women to manage their safety in public spaces. These behavioral changes, while rational responses to perceived threats, collectively diminish women’s freedom of movement and reinforce existing gender inequalities in urban environments.
Socio-Cultural and Patriarchal Underpinnings of Mobility Restrictions
Beyond the immediate threats to physical safety, women’s mobility in metro transit is profoundly shaped by deeply ingrained socio-cultural norms and patriarchal structures. These societal frameworks often dictate women’s roles, responsibilities, and acceptable behaviors, creating a complex web of invisible barriers that limit their access to and experience of public transport. The literature consistently demonstrates that these norms are not merely abstract concepts but have tangible impacts on women’s travel patterns, mode choices, and vulnerability to harassment.
Patriarchal Norms and Victim-Blaming: A Toxic Foundation
Patriarchal societal norms form a “toxic base” that underpins many of the mobility barriers women face, particularly in regions like South and Southeast Asia, as articulated by Sil et al. (2023). These norms not only allow for severe forms of sexual harassment to occur in public transport but also foster an environment where women experience victim-blaming if incidents are reported. This societal response discourages reporting, perpetuates impunity for perpetrators, and reinforces the idea that women are responsible for their own safety, rather than society being responsible for ensuring it. The implication is that women must constantly modify their behavior to avoid harassment, rather than expecting a safe environment.
Even in countries with strong reputations for gender equality, such as Sweden, patriarchal norms persist, delimiting women’s use and access to public space and public transport, as Lindkvist (2024) argues. Despite policy recommendations and legislation aimed at counteracting discrimination, women’s fear of traveling by public transport, especially in the evenings, indicates that these deeply ingrained norms continue to influence societal expectations and behaviors. This highlights that patriarchal structures are not confined to specific geographical regions but are a global phenomenon, albeit with varying degrees of overtness and impact. The “mythical mantra” of gender equality in Sweden, as described by Lindkvist (2024), masks the underlying reality that women still bear a disproportionate burden of ensuring their safety in public spaces.
Cultural and Social Pressures: Shaping Choices and Destinations
Cultural and social pressures exert significant influence on women’s travel choices and destination selections. These pressures often stem from expectations regarding women’s roles within the family and society, their perceived vulnerability, and the need to maintain social propriety. In Jerusalem, Palestinian women face cultural and social pressures that, alongside fear and exclusion, pose significant limitations to their public transport use and destination choice, as revealed by Kerzhner et al. (2018). These pressures can manifest as expectations to avoid certain areas, travel with male escorts, or limit travel during specific times, all of which curtail their autonomy and access to opportunities.
Similarly, in the Dammam Metropolitan Region of Saudi Arabia, women’s mobility has been historically limited and challenged due to socio-cultural barriers, as discussed by Al-Rashid et al. (2020). The study found a predominant preference for taxis for shopping and leisure activities, partly due to poor public transport service but also because privacy and a sense of insecurity often become concerns when women travel alone or with children. This illustrates how cultural expectations around women’s public presence and interactions can push them towards less sustainable and often more expensive private transport options, even when public transport might otherwise be available. The absence of gender-responsive design and infrastructure in public buses further exacerbates these socio-cultural barriers, forcing women into choices that compromise their privacy and security.
Gendered Roles and Trip Chaining: Complex Mobility Patterns
Women’s mobility patterns are often more complex and varied than men’s, largely due to their disproportionate responsibilities for household management, childcare, and caregiving. This leads to “trip chaining,” where multiple short trips are combined for different purposes, often involving different destinations and modes of transport. Pourhashem et al. (2022), in their study across eight European countries, highlights that women and men often do not experience equal mobility opportunities, and gender plays a significant role in transport planning, particularly for addressing individual mobility needs. Their research implies that women’s perception of enjoyable travel time and perceived mood from travel experience would increase the tendency of ridesharing and using private motorized modes as passengers, suggesting a preference for modes that offer greater flexibility or perceived safety for their complex travel needs. They also found that women have a higher perception of the worthwhileness of travel time for walking compared to men, which, while seemingly positive for sustainability, can also indicate inequalities in access to faster, more convenient modes.
Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken (2020), examining Santiago, Chile, underlines the importance of considering non-work trip purposes and achieving better land-use combinations to accommodate care-oriented trips. Women account for a disproportionately high number of walking trips, which, while contributing to environmental sustainability, often signifies significant inequalities in access to efficient public transport. This suggests that environmental and economic sustainability gains might come at a high social cost for women unless specific measures are taken to address their unique travel patterns. Hail & McQuaid (2021) further elaborates on this, noting that women generally have varying and complex mobility patterns compared to men and suffer disproportionate fairness issues when using transport. Factors such as childcare responsibilities directly influence their transport choices and experiences, often leading to higher costs or longer travel times.
The paper by Hortelano et al. (2021), focusing on a European perspective, also identifies gender differences in accessibility to transport modes and safety/security when traveling. It emphasizes the need for planners and policymakers to consider all impacts on gender equality to achieve sustainable and inclusive transport. This body of work collectively demonstrates that women’s mobility is not just about getting from point A to point B, but about navigating a complex landscape of societal expectations, care responsibilities, and perceived risks, all of which are deeply embedded in socio-cultural and patriarchal norms. Addressing these underlying issues is crucial for creating truly equitable and inclusive transport systems.
Infrastructural Deficiencies and Service Quality Gaps
Beyond the socio-cultural and safety dimensions, tangible infrastructural deficiencies and gaps in service quality within metro transit systems present significant practical barriers to women’s mobility. These issues, ranging from poorly designed stations and vehicles to inadequate routes and unreliable services, directly impact women’s accessibility, comfort, and safety, often exacerbating the challenges posed by patriarchal norms and fear.
Poor Infrastructure and Inadequate Service Levels
Many public transport systems, particularly in developing countries, suffer from fundamental infrastructural shortcomings and low service levels that disproportionately affect women. Kerzhner et al. (2018) highlights that for Palestinian women in Jerusalem, poor infrastructure and low levels of public transportation, alongside ethnic segregation and a hostile social climate, create multifaceted barriers to daily travel. This includes insufficient routes, infrequent services, and a general lack of investment in public transport, forcing women to rely on less safe or less convenient alternatives. Similarly, in the Dammam Metropolitan Region of Saudi Arabia, the limited availability of public transportation leads to increased demand for private vehicles, as public transport services are characterized by limited operational routes, hours, and infrastructure, as observed by Al-Rashid et al. (2020). This forces women, who historically have had limited mobility options, to use taxis, often with concerns about privacy and insecurity.
Overcrowding is another critical service quality issue that significantly impacts women. Roy et al. (2024) vividly describes how negotiating everyday overcrowded, unsafe, and unreliable conditions in Kolkata is a major barrier to accessing public transport mobility for women. These conditions trigger negative emotions and produce emotional ordeals, increasing travel time and costs, and ultimately restricting mobility. The sense of despair arising from such conditions often compels women to adjust, accept, or even opt out of public transport. Sil et al. (2023) also identifies poor service quality, alongside sexual harassment and patriarchal norms, as a key barrier for women in South and Southeast Asia, emphasizing the combination of physical discomfort and psychological stress.
The physical environment of transit spaces, such as railway stations, also plays a crucial role in perceived safety and actual victimization. Ceccato et al. (2024b)’s study in Sweden reveals that lack of staff and poor maintenance of stations are significant attributes associated with lower levels of safety perceived by passengers, as well as higher levels of crime and panhandling. For passengers with reduced mobility, the station environment directly affects their likelihood of being victimized. This underscores that even in more developed contexts, basic infrastructural and operational shortcomings can create vulnerabilities. Ali et al. (2025)’s research in Hyderabad, India, further supports this, indicating that infrastructural characteristics like the presence of CCTV and adequate lighting at transit stops and within public transport vehicles are the most important attributes influencing women’s safety and security perceptions. Buses were identified as the most common location for physical harassment, particularly touching, with overcrowding being a main concern, directly linking service quality to safety.
Lack of Gender-Responsive Design and Planning
A significant underlying problem is the historical failure to incorporate a gender perspective into transport planning and infrastructure design. Transport systems have often been designed based on a male-centric model of commuting, overlooking the distinct travel patterns, needs, and safety concerns of women. Al-Rashid et al. (2020) argues that due to the absence of a gender-responsive design and infrastructure in the Dammam Metropolitan Region, women are forced to use taxis, despite concerns about privacy and insecurity. This highlights how the physical environment itself can be a barrier when it does not cater to women’s specific requirements, such as adequate lighting, visible staff, or segregated spaces if desired.
Panjwani (2018), focusing on Karāchī’s public transport policy, points out that the transportation sector is highly gender discriminatory, especially in developing countries, affecting the mobility of women and third genders. The existing shortcomings in public transport systems, which fail to be inclusive and need-based, not only affect mobility but also shape how women occupy public spheres. The paper advocates for an urgent focus on a more inclusive, need-based public transportation system, drawing on principles of gender mainstreaming. This involves considering issues of inclusion, accessibility, safety, and mobility from a gendered perspective in urban planning and city development.
The broader implications of non-gender-responsive design are also discussed by Singh & Choudhury (2025), who argue that urban mobility systems often fail to address the needs of vulnerable populations, particularly women and the elderly. Their systematic literature review identifies a need for urban design interventions that promote gender- and age-inclusive mobility, including universal accessibility, pedestrian-friendly infrastructure, and gender-sensitive planning. The proposed “Pink Map” tool aims to address this by mapping safe and accessible urban spaces for women, emphasizing the need for data-driven decision-making and technology-enabled safety solutions. Thaher (2024), in the context of Amman, Jordan, also stresses the non-neutral nature of transportation systems and advocates for integrating women’s voices, needs, and preferences in planning processes to create inclusive, equitable, and well-being-oriented urban transport systems. This collective body of work underscores that infrastructural deficiencies are not merely technical problems but are often symptoms of a deeper systemic failure to integrate gender considerations into the very fabric of transport planning and design.
Policy, Planning, and Data Gaps
The persistent barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit are not solely attributable to individual behaviors or infrastructural shortcomings; they are often deeply embedded in systemic policy and planning deficiencies. A significant body of literature points to the lack of gender-sensitive policies, inadequate enforcement, and critical gaps in data collection as fundamental issues that perpetuate mobility inequalities for women. Addressing these systemic failures is crucial for fostering truly inclusive and equitable urban transport systems.
Discriminatory Planning Policies and Lack of Inclusive Policies
Historically, transport planning has largely adopted a gender-neutral or male-centric approach, failing to acknowledge and address the distinct mobility patterns, needs, and safety concerns of women. Kerzhner et al. (2018), in their study of Palestinian women in Jerusalem, identifies discriminatory planning policies as a significant barrier, alongside poor infrastructure and low service levels. Such policies can manifest in routes that do not serve women’s typical trip chains (e.g., combining work, childcare, and shopping), or in a lack of investment in public transport in areas predominantly inhabited by women.
Sil et al. (2023) highlights that women’s mobility barriers in South and Southeast Asian countries often originate from the lack of adequate inclusive policies and protection laws from authorities. Despite international organizations providing guidelines for national policymakers, the implementation and effectiveness of these guidelines remain limited. This suggests a disconnect between global recommendations and local realities, where policy frameworks fail to translate into tangible improvements for women on the ground. Panjwani (2018) explicitly states that the transportation sector, particularly in developing countries like Pakistan, is highly gender discriminatory due to inequities in the provision and accessibility of public services. The failure of federal budget allocation towards transportation contributes to profound gender disparities, affecting not only mobility but also women’s occupation of public spheres. The paper advocates for mainstreaming gender in public transport policy to create a more inclusive, need-based system.
Even in contexts like Sweden, which has a political goal of gender equality, Lindkvist (2024) notes that despite policy recommendations and legislation, women still fear traveling by public transport. This indicates that policy alone is insufficient if it does not effectively counteract deeply entrenched patriarchal norms that continue to control women’s everyday transportation. Hortelano et al. (2021), providing a European perspective, emphasizes that to achieve sustainable and inclusive transport, planners and policymakers must consider all impacts on gender equality. The paper reviews EU policy initiatives and regulations, assessing progress and identifying challenges, suggesting that while policy frameworks exist, their comprehensive implementation and impact on women’s real-world experiences still face significant hurdles. Thaher (2024) further reinforces this by arguing for gender-conscious assessments within urban transport planning, emphasizing the non-neutral nature of transportation systems and the need to depart from one-size-fits-all approaches.
Absence of Gender-Disaggregated Data and Evidence-Based Policy
A critical impediment to developing effective gender-responsive transport policies is the widespread absence of gender-disaggregated data. Without specific data on women’s travel patterns, experiences of harassment, perceived safety, and unmet needs, policymakers struggle to formulate evidence-based interventions. Joshi et al. (2022), focusing on South Asia, emphasizes the importance of institutionalizing gender-disaggregated data to inform interventions. Their research, based on in-depth interviews with women in Indian cities, highlights the spectrum of barriers and the need for policies that are grounded in women’s lived experiences. Without such data, policy decisions risk being based on assumptions or generalized statistics that do not reflect the unique challenges faced by women.
Thaher (2024), in the context of Amman, Jordan, underscores the need for strengthening data collection and management to inform gender-sensitive sustainable urban transport planning. The study’s use of the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) with stakeholder perceptions to quantify indicators like safety concerns and citizen satisfaction demonstrates a move towards more nuanced data collection, but also highlights the existing gaps in comprehensive, gender-specific data. Singh & Choudhury (2025) also stresses the need for data-driven decision-making in designing inclusive cities. Their proposal for a “Pink Map” tool, leveraging GIS mapping, community participation, and real-time feedback, exemplifies how technology can be used to gather and visualize gender-specific mobility data, thereby informing urban planning and policy. The lack of such granular, gender-specific data across many regions means that the true extent and nature of women’s mobility barriers remain under-documented, hindering targeted policy responses.
Institutional Sensitization and Enforcement
Even when policies are in place, their effectiveness is often undermined by a lack of institutional sensitization and inadequate enforcement. This includes the need to sensitize institutions such as law enforcement on gender equity and women’s rights to the city, as advocated by Joshi et al. (2022). If authorities, including transport personnel and police, are not adequately trained to understand and respond to women’s safety concerns, or if they perpetuate victim-blaming, policies designed to protect women will fail in practice. Kerzhner et al. (2018)’s policy implications include immediate changes to symbology and behavior of transport personnel, recognizing that the attitudes and actions of those managing and operating public transport systems significantly impact women’s experiences.
Ali et al. (2025)’s study in Hyderabad, India, found that the presence of police and family opinion significantly influenced women’s perceptions of public transport safety and security. This suggests that visible enforcement and a supportive social environment are crucial for enhancing women’s sense of safety. The study highlights the need for planning agencies to adopt necessary enforcement features such as increased police patrolling and formulating operational policies that consider the specific needs of women commuters. Yasar & Basaran (2025) also points out that current security infrastructure in public transportation often fails to equally address the safety needs of men and women, despite existing security measures. This indicates a gap not just in policy formulation, but in the practical implementation and enforcement of security protocols that are truly gender-responsive. The challenge lies in moving beyond superficial measures to create a culture of safety and accountability within transport institutions, ensuring that policies are not just written but actively and equitably enforced.
Methodological Approaches and Research Contributions
The diverse and complex nature of barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit necessitates a variety of research methodologies to capture the full spectrum of experiences, perceptions, and systemic issues. The literature reviewed employs a rich array of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods approaches, each offering unique strengths in understanding this multifaceted problem. A comparative analysis of these methodologies reveals how they collectively contribute to a more holistic understanding, while also highlighting their individual advantages and limitations.
Qualitative Insights: Unveiling Lived Experiences and Emotions
Qualitative research methods are particularly powerful in delving into the subjective experiences, perceptions, and emotional dimensions of women’s mobility. These approaches allow researchers to gather rich, in-depth data that quantitative methods might overlook, providing nuanced insights into the “why” behind observed behaviors.
In-depth Interviews are a cornerstone of qualitative research in this field, enabling researchers to explore personal narratives and detailed accounts of women’s encounters with public transport. Kerzhner et al. (2018), for instance, based their research on eight in-depth interviews with Palestinian women in East Jerusalem, shedding light on individual needs and decisions in an exclusionary urban environment. These interviews revealed how cultural and social pressures, exclusion, and fear significantly limit public transport use and destination choice. Similarly, Joshi et al. (2022)’s findings on gender-responsive transport policies in South Asia are based on in-depth interviews with women participants in Delhi and Kolkata, highlighting the spectrum of barriers and women’s strategies for constructing safety. Al-Rashid et al. (2020) utilized structured interviews as part of their survey campaign in the Dammam Metropolitan Region to understand the gender experience of public transport users and their aspirations. Lindkvist (2024) explored mobility practices, strategies, experiences, reliability, affordability, and comfort through qualitative interviews in Sweden, revealing how safety concerns affect women’s travel choices even in a gender-equal society. Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021) also employed interviews (N=11) alongside a survey to detail the pervasiveness of gender-based violence in Saltillo, Mexico, capturing experiences of re-victimization and psychological effects. King et al. (2021) analyzed qualitative data from women in Bangladesh to examine travel patterns and experiences, revealing multiple threats to safety and high levels of anxiety. Panjwani (2018) conducted first-hand interviews with local conductors, bus drivers, and passengers in Karāchī to highlight specific mobility challenges. Finally, Beck (2024) used the “Commented Walk” method to observe mobility conditions and challenges for women domestic workers in the Metropolitan Region of Curitiba, offering a direct, experiential insight into their daily commutes.
Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) are another valuable qualitative tool, allowing for the exploration of shared experiences and collective perceptions within a group setting. Kerzhner et al. (2018) included 38 participants in focus groups to complement their interviews, providing a broader understanding of common barriers. Kacharo et al. (2022) utilized 24 focus group discussion members in Hawassa city, Ethiopia, to qualitatively analyze data on women and girls’ safety and security in public transport, using narrative analysis to identify types of violence and contributing factors. FGDs can be particularly effective in uncovering social norms and collective coping strategies.
Visual Surveys and Affective Realm Analysis offer innovative ways to capture non-verbal and emotional dimensions. Roy et al. (2024)’s study in Kolkata used visual surveys alongside in-depth interviews to delve into the “affective realm” of women’s commutes. This approach allowed them to comprehend how affects and emotions shape accessibility, acceptability, and affordability of public transport, moving beyond a purely cognitive understanding of barriers. The strength of qualitative methods lies in their ability to provide rich, contextualized data, giving voice to women’s experiences and uncovering the nuanced interplay of factors that quantitative data alone might miss. However, their generalizability can be limited due to smaller sample sizes and context-specific findings.
Quantitative Analysis: Identifying Patterns and Significant Factors
Quantitative research methods are essential for identifying statistically significant patterns, correlations, and the prevalence of specific barriers across larger populations. These approaches provide empirical evidence that can inform policy and planning at a broader scale.
Surveys and Questionnaires are widely used to collect data from a large number of respondents, allowing for statistical analysis. Pourhashem et al. (2022) based their empirical analysis on a multinomial regression model of 1406 female and 1486 male travelers in eight European countries, using survey data to scrutinize differences in perception of travel time value and mode choice behavior. This large-scale data allowed them to identify significant factors influencing travel behavior across genders. Al-Rashid et al. (2020) launched a survey campaign (structured interviews and online questionnaires) in the Dammam Metropolitan Region, taking four different types of respondents into account to understand gender experiences and aspirations. Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021) conducted a survey (N=611) with transit users in Saltillo, Mexico, to quantify the pervasiveness of gender-based violence. Kacharo et al. (2022) obtained data from 199 randomly selected respondents (36.7% women and 63.3% girls) in Hawassa city, Ethiopia, employing binary logistic regression for quantitative data analysis to identify factors contributing to violence. Ceccato et al. (2024b) utilized exploratory data analysis and logistic regression models to assess patterns of victimization and safety perceptions among railway passengers in Sweden, showing how transit environments impact safety. Ceccato et al. (2024a) further used chi-square analysis and regression models on survey data from railway passengers in Sweden to explore women’s safety experiences, particularly young women in diverse rural and urban environments. Ali et al. (2025) surveyed 583 randomly selected women commuters in Hyderabad, India, using binary logit and ordered probit models to analyze factors influencing transit safety and security at transit stops, identifying infrastructural characteristics as crucial. Thaher (2024) employed the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) by surveying 38 stakeholders and decision-makers in Amman, Jordan, to quantify key accessibility, safety, and livability indicators, revealing critical insights into the impact of gender on mobility patterns.
The strength of quantitative methods lies in their ability to provide generalizable findings, identify causal relationships (with appropriate statistical models), and measure the scale of problems. They are crucial for evidence-based policy-making. However, they can sometimes miss the depth and nuance of individual experiences, which qualitative methods are better equipped to capture.
Mixed-Methods Approaches: A Holistic Understanding
Recognizing the limitations of relying solely on one type of methodology, several studies adopt a mixed-methods approach, combining qualitative and quantitative data collection and analysis. This allows researchers to leverage the strengths of both, providing a more comprehensive and robust understanding of the complex issue of women’s mobility barriers.
Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021), for example, used a mixed-method approach that included both a survey (N=611) and interviews (N=11) to detail the pervasiveness of gender-based violence in Saltillo, Mexico. The survey provided statistical evidence of the problem’s scale, while the interviews offered rich narratives of individual experiences and psychological impacts. Similarly, King et al. (2021) analyzed both qualitative and quantitative data to examine travel patterns and experiences among 200 women in Bangladesh, revealing multiple threats to safety and high levels of anxiety. This combination allowed them to identify both the prevalence of threats and the deep emotional toll they take. Ali et al. (2025) also employed a mixed-method approach, combining a survey of 583 women commuters with econometric models (binary logit and ordered probit) and qualitative content analysis of commuters’ statements on harassment experiences. This allowed for the identification of significant infrastructural factors influencing safety perceptions, alongside a nuanced understanding of the types and locations of harassment.
The advantage of mixed-methods research is its capacity to provide both breadth and depth, validating quantitative findings with qualitative insights and vice versa. This triangulation of data sources strengthens the credibility of findings and offers a more complete picture of the barriers women face. While more resource-intensive, mixed-methods approaches are increasingly recognized as essential for tackling complex social issues like gendered mobility inequalities, as they allow for a comprehensive understanding of both the structural and experiential dimensions of the problem.
Conclusion
The literature reviewed unequivocally demonstrates that women’s mobility in metro transit is severely hampered by a complex interplay of barriers, ranging from overt threats to deeply ingrained societal norms and systemic planning deficiencies. These obstacles are not isolated but rather interconnected, creating a challenging and often unsafe environment that restricts women’s access to essential urban opportunities and undermines their fundamental right to equitable mobility.
The pervasive threat of safety and security concerns emerges as a paramount barrier. Gender-based violence and sexual harassment are alarmingly common experiences for women across diverse geographical contexts, from South and Southeast Asia (King et al., 2021; Sil et al., 2023) to Latin America (Infante-Vargas & Boyer, 2021) and Africa (Kacharo et al., 2022). This direct violence, coupled with a generalized fear of potential harm, acts as a powerful deterrent, compelling women to adopt restrictive behavioral adaptations such as avoiding certain routes, times, or modes of transport (Kerzhner et al., 2018; Yasar & Basaran, 2025). The psychological toll of this constant vigilance and the emotional ordeals of unsafe commutes are significant, impacting women’s mental well-being and overall quality of life (Roy et al., 2024).
Underpinning many of these safety issues are deeply entrenched socio-cultural and patriarchal norms. These norms perpetuate victim-blaming, create hostile public environments, and dictate women’s roles and responsibilities, leading to complex travel patterns characterized by trip chaining for caregiving duties (Hail & McQuaid, 2021; Pourhashem et al., 2022; Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken, 2020). Even in societies with strong gender equality reputations, such as Sweden, patriarchal norms continue to influence women’s perceived safety and access to public spaces (Lindkvist, 2024). Cultural and social pressures further shape women’s mode choices and destination selections, often pushing them towards less sustainable or more expensive private transport options to ensure privacy and security (Al-Rashid et al., 2020).
Infrastructural deficiencies and service quality gaps exacerbate these challenges. Poor infrastructure, inadequate lighting, limited routes and operational hours, and severe overcrowding are common issues, particularly in developing countries (Ali et al., 2025; Al-Rashid et al., 2020; Kerzhner et al., 2018; Roy et al., 2024). Crucially, the historical lack of gender-responsive design and planning means that transport systems often fail to meet women’s specific needs, further contributing to their reliance on less sustainable modes and increasing their vulnerability (Al-Rashid et al., 2020; Panjwani, 2018; Singh & Choudhury, 2025). The physical characteristics of transit environments, such as poorly maintained stations and lack of staff, directly impact perceived safety and victimization risk (Ceccato et al., 2024b).
Finally, systemic policy, planning, and data gaps represent fundamental barriers. Discriminatory planning policies and a lack of inclusive frameworks often overlook women’s distinct mobility needs, despite international guidelines (Joshi et al., 2022; Sil et al., 2023). The absence of gender-disaggregated data hinders evidence-based policy-making, making it difficult to understand the true extent and nature of women’s mobility challenges (Joshi et al., 2022; Thaher, 2024). Furthermore, inadequate institutional sensitization and enforcement, particularly among law enforcement and transport personnel, undermine the effectiveness of existing policies, perpetuating a cycle of insecurity and impunity (Joshi et al., 2022; Kerzhner et al., 2018).
Research Gaps and Future Directions
Despite the growing body of literature, several critical research gaps remain, offering fertile ground for future inquiry:
1.	Intersectionality of Barriers: While some studies touch upon intersectionality (e.g., Ceccato et al. (2024b) on LGBTQI+/Non-binary/Other women; Ceccato et al. (2024a) on young women), there is a need for more in-depth research exploring how different social identities (e.g., race, class, disability, age, migrant status) intersect to create unique and compounded mobility barriers for women. How do these intersections influence experiences of violence, access to resources, and behavioral adaptations?
2.	Effectiveness of Interventions: Many studies highlight the problems, but fewer rigorously evaluate the long-term effectiveness of specific interventions (e.g., women-only solutions, technology-based safety apps, infrastructural improvements, policy changes). Future research should focus on impact assessments, comparing different strategies across contexts to identify best practices.
3.	Longitudinal Studies: Most studies provide a snapshot of women’s experiences. Longitudinal research could track changes in mobility patterns, safety perceptions, and the impact of interventions over time, offering a dynamic understanding of the issue.
4.	Broader Geographical Representation: While the current literature covers diverse regions, there are still significant gaps, particularly in parts of Africa, Eastern Europe, and indigenous communities globally. Expanding geographical scope would provide a more universal understanding of context-specific and universal barriers.
5.	Economic and Health Impacts: While some papers mention economic and emotional repercussions (Infante-Vargas & Boyer, 2021; Roy et al., 2024), more dedicated research is needed to quantify the full economic costs (e.g., lost wages, healthcare expenses) and long-term health impacts (e.g., chronic stress, mental health issues) of restricted and unsafe mobility for women.
6.	Role of Men and Boys: While the focus is on women’s experiences, understanding the role of men and boys in perpetuating or preventing harassment, and their perceptions of women’s mobility, is crucial for comprehensive solutions. Research on engaging men as allies and changing male behaviors could be transformative.
7.	Autonomous Vehicles and Future Mobility: Yasar & Basaran (2025) briefly explores the potential future impacts of autonomous vehicles on in-vehicle security. This area requires extensive research to proactively design future mobility solutions that are inherently safe and equitable for women, rather than retrofitting solutions to existing problems.
Future research should continue to employ mixed-methods approaches to capture both the breadth and depth of women’s mobility experiences. There is a strong call for more participatory research, actively involving women in the design and evaluation of transport solutions, ensuring that their voices and needs are central to the planning process (Singh & Choudhury, 2025; Thaher, 2024). Furthermore, a rights-based approach to framing interventions is essential, recognizing women’s right to the city and safe, equitable mobility as fundamental human rights (Joshi et al., 2022; King et al., 2021). Ultimately, achieving gender equity in urban transport requires not just policy changes, but a fundamental shift in societal attitudes and a conscious commitment to designing and managing public spaces and services that are truly inclusive and safe for all women.

Introduction
Urban mobility is a fundamental enabler of social inclusion, economic participation, and overall quality of life, yet for women worldwide, access to safe, efficient, and equitable public transportation remains a significant challenge. Metro transit systems, while designed to facilitate mass movement, often inadvertently or explicitly perpetuate existing gender inequalities, creating a complex web of barriers that restrict women’s mobility. These restrictions are not merely inconveniences; they profoundly impact women’s ability to pursue education, employment, healthcare, and social activities, thereby undermining their autonomy and contributing to broader societal disparities. The issue of women’s mobility in public transport is increasingly recognized as a critical area of academic inquiry and policy intervention, moving beyond a simplistic understanding of transport as gender-neutral to a nuanced appreciation of its gendered dimensions.
The existing literature underscores that women’s travel patterns, needs, and experiences differ significantly from those of men, often characterized by more complex trip chains, care-oriented journeys, and a heightened perception of risk (Pourhashem et al., 2022; Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken, 2020; VUJIČIĆ et al., 2025). These differences are not inherent but are shaped by societal roles, cultural expectations, and the design and operation of transport systems themselves. Consequently, a failure to account for these gendered realities in transport planning and policy can lead to systems that are inherently exclusionary and unsafe for women. This paper aims to provide a comprehensive literature review on the barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit, synthesizing insights from a diverse body of research. It seeks to identify the primary categories of these barriers, analyze their manifestations across different geographical and socio-cultural contexts, critically compare the methodologies employed to study them, and ultimately highlight key research gaps and future directions for creating truly inclusive public transport systems. By drawing upon a range of studies, from those focusing on South and Southeast Asia to Europe, the Middle East, and Latin America, this review endeavors to offer a holistic understanding of a global challenge with localized specificities.
The Pervasiveness of Safety and Security Concerns in Metro Transit
One of the most consistently highlighted and impactful barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit is the pervasive issue of safety and security, often manifesting as gender-based violence (GBV) and harassment. This concern is not confined to specific regions but is a global phenomenon, deeply influencing women’s travel choices, behaviors, and overall quality of life. The literature unequivocally demonstrates that the fear and reality of violence significantly deter women from utilizing public transport, thereby limiting their access to opportunities and reinforcing existing inequalities.
Gender-Based Violence and Harassment as Primary Deterrents
Gender-based violence and harassment are identified as primary threats to women’s safety in public transport across numerous studies. Sil, Chowdhury, and Thoreau Sil et al. (2023), in their critical review of women’s mobility barriers in South and Southeast Asia, explicitly state that severe forms of sexual harassment are prevalent when women ride public transport, exacerbated by underlying patriarchal societal norms. This finding is echoed by Roy, Bailey, and van Noorloos Roy et al. (2024), who delve into the “affects and emotions” of everyday commutes in Kolkata, highlighting that negotiating overcrowded, unsafe, and unreliable conditions is a major barrier that triggers negative emotions, including despair. Similarly, Joshi et al. Joshi et al. (2022), focusing on South Asia, emphasize that challenges such as sexual harassment still prevail, underscoring the need for gender-responsive transport policies. The experiences of women in Bangladesh, as explored by King et al. King et al. (2021), reveal that women face multiple threats to their safety, including GBV, harassment, and crime, leading to high levels of anxiety and fear. Despite these circumstances, women are often “captive travelers,” forced to make choices based on price and availability rather than safety, attempting to mitigate risks by altering travel patterns and restricting frequency. Panjwani Panjwani (2018) further reinforces this in the context of Karāchī, Pakistan, noting that the transportation sector is highly gender discriminatory, affecting the mobility of women and third genders who face inequities in safety and accessibility.
The forms of violence are diverse, ranging from physical and sexual to psychological, as detailed by Kacharo, Teshome, and Woltamo Kacharo et al. (2022) in their study in Hawassa city, Ethiopia. Their findings indicate that over half of women and girls experienced more than one type of violence while using public transport, with age, marital status, type of public transport, travel time, facilities, and management significantly contributing to these incidents. This comprehensive view of violence extends beyond overt physical acts to include subtle yet equally damaging forms of harassment and intimidation. Infante-Vargas and Boyer Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021) provide a detailed account of the pervasiveness of GBV against women users of public transport in Saltillo, Mexico, revealing that women experience violence as an everyday reality, coupled with issues of re-victimization and profound psychological effects. Their research argues that the impacts extend beyond the immediate event, limiting mobility and incurring financial and emotional repercussions. These findings collectively paint a grim picture of public transport spaces as sites where women are disproportionately vulnerable to various forms of violence, making safety a paramount concern that dictates their mobility choices.
The perception of security-related risk is a critical factor influencing travel decisions, yet existing security infrastructure often fails to address the distinct safety needs of women. Yasar and Basaran Yasar & Basaran (2025) argue that women experience criminal and violent acts more frequently despite current security measures, leading to behavioral adaptations such as trip avoidance, mode shifts, or destination changes. These adaptations, in turn, create barriers to accessing essential services and opportunities, thereby undermining transportation equity. Ali et al. Ali et al. (2025), investigating Indian women commuters’ safety and security perceptions in Hyderabad, found that infrastructural characteristics like CCTV and lighting at transit stops and within vehicles were crucial. Their study also highlighted buses as common locations for physical harassment, particularly touching, with overcrowding being a major concern. This suggests that while the presence of security features is important, their effectiveness is often compromised by design flaws and operational realities.
Even in countries with strong reputations for gender equality, such as Sweden, women continue to fear traveling by public transport, especially in the evenings, indicating the persistence of patriarchal norms. Lindkvist Lindkvist (2024) reveals that women’s travel choices are significantly affected by safety concerns, leading to necessary adaptations to perceived threatening situations. This demonstrates that the issue transcends specific cultural contexts, suggesting a deeper, more universal challenge rooted in gendered power dynamics. Ceccato, Sundling, and Gliori Ceccato et al. (2024b) further elaborate on this, assessing patterns of victimization and safety perceptions at railway stations in Sweden. Their subsequent work Ceccato et al. (2024a) highlights that while rural women may feel safer than urban counterparts, they are more willing to take precautionary measures, such as traveling with someone in the evening or avoiding certain stations, underscoring the persistent need for vigilance and adaptation regardless of the environment. These studies collectively emphasize that safety is not merely the absence of crime but a complex interplay of physical environment, social dynamics, and individual perceptions, all of which disproportionately affect women.
Psychological Impacts and Fear-Based Mobility Restrictions
Beyond the direct experience of violence, the psychological impacts of perceived insecurity significantly restrict women’s mobility. The constant threat of harassment or violence instills fear and anxiety, compelling women to alter their travel behaviors, limit their destinations, or avoid public transport altogether. Kerzhner, Kaplan, and Silverman Kerzhner et al. (2018), in their study of Palestinian women’s mobility in Jerusalem, found that cultural and social pressures, coupled with a sense of exclusion and fear, pose significant limitations to public transport use and destination choice. Many women internalize expectations of violence and exclusionary cues, which serve as fear-based barriers. Their responses include avoiding certain areas, minimizing travel, or, in some cases, adopting private cars as a safer alternative. This internalization of fear transforms public spaces, including transit, into arenas of potential threat, forcing women to constantly assess and manage risk.
Roy, Bailey, and van Noorloos Roy et al. (2024) explicitly discuss the “disruptive affects” of overcrowded, unsafe, and unreliable conditions, which produce emotional ordeals and a sense of despair. This despair compels women to adjust, accept, or even opt out of public transport, highlighting the profound emotional toll that inadequate and unsafe transit systems exact. King et al. King et al. (2021) similarly report high levels of anxiety and fear among women using paid transport in Bangladesh, even when they are “captive travelers” with limited alternatives. The psychological burden of constantly being alert, assessing risks, and anticipating potential threats is a significant, yet often overlooked, barrier to mobility. Infante-Vargas and Boyer Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021) further elaborate on the psychological effects of violent episodes, including re-victimization, which can have long-lasting impacts on women’s well-being and their willingness to use public transport.
The cumulative effect of these psychological barriers is a reduction in women’s spatial freedom and autonomy. They are forced to make travel choices not based on efficiency or convenience, but on perceived safety, often leading to longer, more expensive, or less direct routes. This self-imposed restriction, driven by fear, effectively shrinks their “activity spaces” and limits their participation in urban life. Lindkvist Lindkvist (2024) notes that women’s travel choices in Sweden are affected by concerns about safety and the necessary adaptation to threatening situations, despite the country’s reputation for gender equality. This suggests that the psychological impact of perceived insecurity is deeply ingrained and transcends socio-economic or developmental contexts, pointing to a fundamental challenge in achieving true gender equity in urban mobility. The “mythical mantra” of gender equality, as Lindkvist puts it, does not erase the lived reality of fear that delimits women’s use and access to public space and public transport.

In the Dammam Metropolitan Region, Saudi Arabia, Al-Rashid et al. Al-Rashid et al. (2020) point to socio-cultural barriers that limit women’s mobility, alongside poor public transport service. The absence of gender-responsive design and infrastructure forces women to use taxis, where privacy and insecurity can still be concerns. This highlights how cultural norms, such as expectations around gender segregation or women’s roles, can intersect with transport provision to create unique barriers. Similarly, in Jerusalem, Kerzhner, Kaplan, and Silverman Kerzhner et al. (2018) describe how ethnic segregation and a hostile social climate compound poor infrastructure, creating multifaceted barriers for Palestinian women. The internalization of expectations of violence and exclusionary linguistic/visual cues are particularly salient here, demonstrating how broader political and social conflicts can directly impact women’s safety in transit.
Even in European contexts, where public transport systems are generally more developed, safety remains a significant issue. Pourhashem et al. Pourhashem et al. (2022), while focusing on travel time perception, implicitly acknowledge that women and men often do not experience equal mobility opportunities. Lindkvist Lindkvist (2024) explicitly states that despite Sweden’s reputation for gender equality, women still fear traveling by public transport, indicating that patriarchal norms persist and influence safety perceptions. Ceccato, Sundling, and Gliori’s work in Sweden Ceccato et al. (2024b); Ceccato et al. This comparative perspective reveals that while the specific drivers of insecurity may differ, the fundamental challenge of ensuring women’s safety in public transit is a shared global concern, requiring context-specific yet universally informed interventions. The common thread across these diverse geographies is the disproportionate burden of fear and insecurity borne by women, which fundamentally shapes their engagement with urban spaces and public services.
Infrastructural Deficiencies and Service Quality Limitations
Beyond the direct threat of violence, the physical characteristics of metro transit infrastructure and the quality of services provided constitute another significant category of barriers to women’s mobility. These issues range from inadequate design and maintenance of stations and vehicles to operational shortcomings such as overcrowding, unreliability, and limited accessibility, all of which disproportionately affect women’s travel experiences and choices.
Inadequate Design and Maintenance
The design and maintenance of public transport infrastructure often fail to consider the specific needs and safety concerns of women, thereby creating environments that are perceived as, or are actually, unsafe and uncomfortable. Sil, Chowdhury, and Thoreau Sil et al. (2023) identify poor service quality as a key barrier for women in South and Southeast Asia, which implicitly includes infrastructural deficiencies. Kerzhner, Kaplan, and Silverman Kerzhner et al. (2018) explicitly mention poor infrastructure and low levels of public transportation as multifaceted barriers for Palestinian women in Jerusalem. This suggests that the physical environment itself, when poorly planned or maintained, can become a source of exclusion.
Al-Rashid et al. Al-Rashid et al. (2020) critically examine the adequacy of supporting infrastructures and interior design of public buses in the Dammam Metropolitan Region, Saudi Arabia. Their findings reveal that the absence of gender-responsive design and infrastructure forces women to opt for taxis, even with associated privacy and insecurity concerns. This highlights a direct link between design choices and women’s mode choice, demonstrating how a lack of attention to gender in infrastructure planning can push women towards less sustainable and potentially less safe alternatives. For instance, the interior design of buses might not offer sufficient privacy or designated spaces, making women feel exposed or vulnerable. Similarly, Joshi et al. Joshi et al. (2022), in their study on gender-responsive transport policies in South Asia, emphasize the importance of infrastructural design as an intervention to construct safety for women. This implies that current designs are often lacking in this regard, contributing to the spectrum of barriers women face from origin to destination.
Ceccato, Sundling, and Gliori Ceccato et al. (2024b) provide empirical evidence from Sweden, showing that passengers’ safety perceptions are significantly affected by the physical and social characteristics of transit environments. Specifically, they found that poor maintenance of railway stations is associated with lower levels of safety perceived by passengers. This suggests that even seemingly minor issues like broken lighting, dirty waiting areas, or lack of visible staff can contribute to an overall sense of insecurity, particularly for women. Ali et al. Ali et al. (2025) reinforce this, stating that infrastructural characteristics such as the presence of CCTV and adequate lighting at transit stops and in public transport vehicles are among the most important attributes influencing women’s safety and security perceptions in Hyderabad, India. The implication is clear: well-lit, clean, and monitored environments are crucial for enhancing women’s sense of safety, and their absence constitutes a significant barrier. Thaher Thaher (2024), in the context of Amman, Jordan, also underscores the importance of safety concerns like air pollution emission and fatality rates, alongside livability indicators, in shaping women’s well-being in urban transport. While not directly about harassment, these factors contribute to the overall quality and safety perception of the transport environment.
Furthermore, the concept of universal accessibility, as highlighted by Singh and Choudhury Singh & Choudhury (2025), is crucial for inclusive mobility. This extends beyond physical accessibility for those with reduced mobility to encompass design features that make spaces feel safe and welcoming for all, including women. The lack of pedestrian-friendly infrastructure and poor last-mile connectivity, also identified by Singh and Choudhury, further compounds the challenges, as women often rely on walking for parts of their journeys, especially for care-oriented trips (Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken, 2020). If the routes to and from metro stations are unsafe, poorly lit, or inaccessible, the entire journey becomes a barrier. Beck Beck (2024), studying women in the Metropolitan Region of Curitiba, Brazil, who use public transport for domestic services, points out limitations in accessibility, inclusion, and safety, emphasizing the need for public policies that consider women’s needs in urban space production and mobility improvement. This holistic view of infrastructure, encompassing not just the transit vehicle or station but the entire journey chain, is essential for understanding the full scope of these barriers.
Operational Shortcomings: Overcrowding, Unreliability, and Limited Access
Beyond static infrastructure, the operational aspects of metro transit systems—such as service frequency, reliability, route coverage, and vehicle capacity—also present significant barriers for women. These operational shortcomings often intersect with safety concerns, exacerbating the challenges women face.
Overcrowding is a frequently cited issue. Roy, Bailey, and van Noorloos Roy et al. (2024) explicitly identify “everyday overcrowded, unsafe, and unreliable conditions” as a major barrier for women in Kolkata, triggering emotional ordeals and compelling them to adjust or opt out. Ali et al. Ali et al. (2025) confirm that bus overcrowdedness was a main concern for women commuters in Hyderabad, India, particularly in relation to physical harassment. Overcrowded spaces provide opportunities for harassment and reduce women’s ability to protect themselves or seek help, making the journey not only uncomfortable but also potentially dangerous.
Unreliability and limited service availability also pose substantial problems. Al-Rashid et al. Al-Rashid et al. (2020) note that poor public transport service in Dammam, characterized by limited operational routes, hours, and infrastructure, leads to a predominant preference for taxis among women for shopping and leisure activities. This lack of reliable and extensive public transport forces women into more expensive or less safe alternatives, directly impacting their mobility and autonomy. King et al. King et al. (2021) highlight that women in Bangladesh are often “captive travelers,” making transport choices based on price, availability, and ease of travel rather than safety, precisely because reliable and safe options are scarce. This scarcity of good quality service means women often have to compromise on safety or convenience.
Furthermore, women’s travel patterns are often more complex than men’s, involving multiple stops for care-related activities (e.g., dropping children at school, shopping, elder care), often referred to as “trip chaining.” Sagaris and Tiznado-Aitken Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken (2020), in their insights from Santiago, Chile, underline the importance of considering non-work trip purposes and achieving better land-use combinations to accommodate care-oriented trips. The existing public transport systems are often designed with a male-centric “commuter” model in mind, focusing on peak-hour, home-to-work trips, which does not align with women’s more dispersed and multi-purpose travel needs. Vujičić et al. VUJIČIĆ et al. (2025), studying daily trip characteristics in Novi Sad, Serbia, found that women do not have precise peak hours, and their trips are more related to daily duties than entertainment, scattered even outside peak hours. This mismatch between system design and women’s travel patterns means that limited routes, infrequent services, or lack of connectivity in off-peak hours disproportionately affect women, making their journeys longer, more arduous, and less efficient.
The cost of public transport can also be a barrier, particularly when safe alternatives are more expensive. Field and Vyborny Field & Vyborny (2022), in Pakistan, found that women’s response to reduced physical mobility constraints was driven by a women-only transport treatment arm, suggesting that safety and social acceptability, rather than simply cost, are key constraints. However, cost remains a factor, especially when women are forced to choose more expensive private options due to public transport deficiencies. Hail and McQuaid Hail & McQuaid (2021) include cost as a significant fairness issue for women using transport, alongside safety and physical design. The economic burden of inadequate public transport can be substantial, particularly for low-income women or those with childcare responsibilities, further limiting their mobility and economic participation.
In summary, the operational shortcomings of metro transit systems, including overcrowding, unreliability, limited routes and hours, and a mismatch with women’s complex travel patterns, combine with infrastructural deficiencies to create a formidable set of barriers. These issues not only make travel less convenient but also directly compromise women’s safety and autonomy, forcing them into difficult choices that often restrict their access to urban opportunities. Addressing these operational and infrastructural challenges requires a fundamental shift towards gender-sensitive planning and design that prioritizes women’s diverse needs and experiences.
The Role of Technology and Physical Interventions
In response to the pervasive safety and security concerns, and to mitigate the impact of infrastructural deficiencies, several studies highlight the potential and actual role of technology and specific physical interventions. These solutions aim to enhance safety, improve service quality, and ultimately promote women’s mobility in metro transit. However, their effectiveness is often contingent on thoughtful implementation and integration within broader policy frameworks.
Joshi et al. Joshi et al. (2022), in their research on gender-responsive transport policies in South Asia, specifically identify technology, women-only solutions, and infrastructural design as key interventions and tools available to women for constructing safety. This suggests a multi-pronged approach where technology can play a crucial role. For instance, mobile applications for reporting harassment, real-time tracking of vehicles, or panic buttons can empower women and provide a sense of security. However, the effectiveness of such technological solutions depends on their accessibility, user-friendliness, and the responsiveness of the authorities to reported incidents.
The presence of surveillance technologies, such as CCTV cameras, is frequently mentioned as a deterrent to crime and a tool for enhancing safety. Ali et al. Ali et al. (2025), studying women commuters in Hyderabad, India, found that the presence of CCTV and adequate lighting at transit stops and in vehicles were the most important attributes influencing women’s safety and security perceptions. This indicates that visible surveillance can contribute significantly to a perceived sense of safety. However, the mere presence of cameras is not sufficient; effective monitoring, rapid response mechanisms, and clear protocols for handling incidents captured on camera are equally vital. Without these, CCTV can become a symbolic gesture rather than a genuine safety measure.
Women-only solutions, such as women-only carriages or buses, are another intervention discussed in the literature. Field and Vyborny Field & Vyborny (2022), in their experimental study in Pakistan, found that women’s response to reduced physical mobility constraints was significantly driven by a women-only transport treatment arm. This suggests that for many women, the provision of spaces explicitly designated for them can address deep-seated concerns about safety and social acceptability, which are often more critical than mere cost considerations. Panjwani Panjwani (2018), in the context of Karāchī, also implicitly supports such interventions by advocating for a more inclusive, need-based public transportation system that addresses the specific challenges faced by women. However, the debate around women-only solutions is complex. While they offer immediate relief and a sense of security for some, critics argue that they can be a temporary fix that segregates women rather than addressing the root causes of harassment and violence in mixed-gender spaces. They might also inadvertently limit women’s choices or reinforce patriarchal norms by implying that women need separate spaces for protection.
Infrastructural design improvements go hand-in-hand with technological interventions. As highlighted by Ali et al. Ali et al. (2025), adequate illumination at transit stops and within public transport vehicles is critical. Well-lit areas reduce opportunities for crime and enhance visibility, making women feel safer. Beyond lighting, the overall design of stations and stops, including clear sightlines, open spaces, and the presence of staff, can significantly impact safety perceptions. Ceccato, Sundling, and Gliori Ceccato et al. (2024b) found that lack of staff and poor maintenance of stations were associated with lower safety perceptions, suggesting that human presence and well-maintained environments are as important as technological solutions. Singh and Choudhury Singh & Choudhury (2025) propose a “Pink Map” as a data-driven urban planning tool to map safe and accessible urban spaces for women and the elderly, leveraging GIS mapping, community participation, and real-time feedback. This innovative approach integrates technology with community input to inform urban design, aiming for a more proactive and responsive planning process.
The future impacts of autonomous vehicles on in-vehicle security for women are also beginning to be explored. Yasar and Basaran Yasar & Basaran (2025) touch upon this, suggesting that while autonomous vehicles might offer new possibilities for in-vehicle security, their implications for gender equity need careful consideration. The design of these future transport modes must proactively integrate gender-sensitive security features to avoid replicating existing barriers.
In conclusion, technology and physical interventions offer promising avenues for addressing women’s mobility barriers in metro transit. From surveillance systems and women-only spaces to improved lighting and innovative planning tools, these solutions can enhance safety and comfort. However, their success hinges on a holistic approach that combines technological advancements with thoughtful design, robust policy frameworks, and a deep understanding of women’s diverse needs and experiences, ensuring that they are not merely superficial fixes but contribute to systemic change.
Socio-Cultural and Patriarchal Underpinnings of Mobility Barriers
Beyond the tangible issues of safety, infrastructure, and service quality, a profound and often invisible layer of barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit stems from deeply entrenched socio-cultural norms and patriarchal structures. These societal expectations and power dynamics shape women’s roles, restrict their spatial freedom, and influence their interactions within public spaces, including public transport. The literature consistently demonstrates that these norms are not merely background factors but active determinants of women’s travel experiences.
Societal Norms and Their Impact on Women’s Travel Choices
Societal norms dictate appropriate behaviors, roles, and spaces for women, often leading to self-imposed or externally enforced restrictions on their mobility. Sil, Chowdhury, and Thoreau Sil et al. (2023) explicitly state that women in South and Southeast Asia encounter unique mobility barriers which are a combination of poor services and “underlying patriarchal societal norms.” These norms form a “toxic base” that allows for sexual harassment and victim-blaming, indicating a direct link between societal values and the prevalence of violence in public transport. This suggests that the problem is not just about individual perpetrators but a broader cultural context that condones or enables such behavior.
Field and Vyborny Field & Vyborny (2022), in their study in Lahore, Pakistan, highlight that in cities with conservative norms or high crime, female workers may face greater restrictions on their physical mobility. Their finding that women’s response to transport services was driven by a women-only treatment arm suggests that “social acceptability,” rather than just cost, is a key constraint. This implies that even if transport is physically available and affordable, societal expectations about women’s presence in public spaces or their interactions with men can deter them from using it. The desire for women-only spaces, therefore, is not just about physical safety but also about navigating social norms that make mixed-gender public spaces uncomfortable or unacceptable for many women.
Al-Rashid et al. Al-Rashid et al. (2020), in Saudi Arabia, similarly point to socio-cultural barriers that limit and challenge women’s mobility in spatio-temporal terms. The absence of gender-responsive design and infrastructure, partly due to these socio-cultural factors, forces women into less ideal transport choices. This illustrates how cultural norms can influence policy and planning decisions, leading to transport systems that do not cater to women’s needs. For instance, cultural expectations regarding women’s privacy or segregation might not be adequately addressed in public transport design, making it less appealing or even forbidden for some women.
Lindkvist Lindkvist (2024) provides a compelling argument that even in Sweden, a country with a strong reputation for gender equality, “patriarchal norms controlling women’s everyday transportation” persist. Despite policy recommendations and legislation, women still fear traveling by public transport, indicating that societal norms can override formal legal frameworks. This “mythical mantra” of gender equality does not erase the lived reality where women’s use and access to public space and public transport are delimited by ingrained patriarchal expectations. This comparative insight is crucial, demonstrating that socio-cultural barriers are not exclusive to developing countries but are a global challenge, albeit with varying degrees of intensity and manifestation.
Cultural Pressures and the Internalization of Exclusion
Cultural pressures often lead to the internalization of expectations of violence or exclusion, which then become self-imposed barriers to mobility. Kerzhner, Kaplan, and Silverman Kerzhner et al. (2018) vividly describe how Palestinian women in Jerusalem internalize expectations of violence and exclusionary linguistic and visual cues. These internalized fears then serve as “fear-based barriers” for public transport use, leading women to avoid certain areas or minimize travel. This process of internalization means that women may restrict their own mobility even in the absence of immediate threats, simply due to a learned understanding of their vulnerability in public spaces.
Roy, Bailey, and van Noorloos Roy et al. (2024) touch upon the “affects and emotions” of everyday commutes, where the “sense of despair” compels women to adjust, accept, or opt out of public transport. This despair is not just a reaction to immediate conditions but also a reflection of a deeper understanding that these conditions are persistent and perhaps unchangeable, rooted in broader societal indifference or patriarchal structures. The emotional burden of constantly navigating unsafe and unreliable conditions, coupled with the knowledge that these are often gendered experiences, contributes to this sense of despair and subsequent self-restriction.
Panjwani Panjwani (2018) argues that constraints to safe, efficient, and comfortable mobility for women in Karāchī are both a cause and an effect of social inequality. This highlights a cyclical relationship where societal norms create unsafe transport, which in turn reinforces social inequality by limiting women’s participation. The failure of federal budget allocation towards transportation, leading to profound gender disparities, is also linked to these underlying societal values that de-prioritize women’s mobility needs. The way space is occupied by women in public spheres is thus shaped by these constraints, leading to a reduced presence and visibility of women in urban life.
Thaher Thaher (2024), in his study on Amman, Jordan, emphasizes the need for interventions that break down barriers and amplify opportunities, thus transforming transportation networks into enablers of women’s empowerment and social participation. This implicitly acknowledges that cultural biases in indicator selection and a lack of stakeholder engagement can perpetuate existing socio-cultural barriers in transport planning. The study advocates for integrating women’s voices, needs, and preferences in planning processes, which is a direct counter to the historical exclusion driven by patriarchal norms.
Intersectionality of Gender with Other Social Factors
The impact of socio-cultural and patriarchal norms is often compounded by other social factors, creating intersectional barriers that further disadvantage women. While not explicitly detailed in every abstract, several studies allude to this complexity. Kerzhner, Kaplan, and Silverman Kerzhner et al. (2018), for instance, mention “ethnic segregation” alongside a hostile social climate as multifaceted barriers for Palestinian women in Jerusalem. This suggests that being a woman from a marginalized ethnic group can lead to a unique set of mobility challenges, where gendered vulnerability intersects with ethnic discrimination.
Ceccato, Sundling, and Gliori Ceccato et al. (2024b) provide direct evidence of intersectionality, finding that “women, but in particular those who identify as LGBTQI+ /Non-binary/Other, run a higher likelihood of feeling less safe” in railway stations in Sweden. This highlights how gender identity, beyond biological sex, can significantly influence safety perceptions and experiences in public transport, indicating that a one-size-fits-all approach to gender-responsive planning is insufficient. Their subsequent work Ceccato et al. (2024a) also calls for “gender-age-sensitive mobility policies, taking into account the intersectionality of transit safety,” particularly for young women in rural-urban contexts. This emphasizes that age, combined with gender and geographical location, creates distinct safety needs and experiences.
Hail and McQuaid Hail & McQuaid (2021), in their discussion of fairness in transport, recognize that fairness principles should apply to all people regardless of sex, gender, or other characteristics. They specifically mention “low-income or childcare responsibilities” as factors that create disproportionate fairness issues for women. This points to the intersection of gender with socio-economic status and caregiving roles, where women, often bearing the primary responsibility for childcare, face compounded challenges related to cost, convenience, and safety when traveling with children. These intersectional considerations are crucial for developing truly equitable transport systems that address the diverse needs of all women.
In conclusion, socio-cultural and patriarchal norms constitute a deep-seated and pervasive set of barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit. These norms influence women’s travel choices, lead to the internalization of fear and exclusion, and intersect with other social factors to create compounded disadvantages. Addressing these barriers requires not only changes in infrastructure and policy but also a fundamental shift in societal attitudes and a conscious effort to challenge and dismantle patriarchal structures that limit women’s freedom of movement.
Policy, Planning, and Governance Gaps in Promoting Inclusive Mobility
A critical dimension of the barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit lies in the systemic failures within policy, planning, and governance frameworks. Despite growing recognition of gender differences in transport, many existing policies and planning practices remain gender-blind or inadequately responsive to women’s specific needs and vulnerabilities. This leads to a perpetuation of exclusionary transport systems that fail to ensure equitable access and safety for women.
Absence of Gender-Responsive Transport Policies
A recurring theme in the literature is the lack of adequate and effectively implemented gender-responsive transport policies. Sil, Chowdhury, and Thoreau Sil et al. (2023) explicitly state that women’s mobility barriers in South and Southeast Asian countries originate from “the lack of adequate inclusive policies and protection laws from authorities.” They note that while international organizations provide guidelines, national policy makers often fall short in developing truly inclusive public transport systems. This highlights a disconnect between global recommendations and local implementation, suggesting that policy frameworks either do not exist, are poorly formulated, or are not rigorously enforced.
Joshi et al. Joshi et al. (2022) directly address this gap by contributing towards “gender-responsive transport policies” for South Asia, emphasizing the need to frame interventions in a rights-based manner. Their research, based on interviews in Indian cities, underscores that despite growing cognizance of gender differences in planning, challenges like sexual harassment persist, indicating that current policies are insufficient. They advocate for institutionalizing gender-disaggregated data to inform interventions, which is a foundational step for evidence-based policy-making that truly reflects women’s experiences.
Al-Rashid et al. Al-Rashid et al. (2020), in their study of the Dammam Metropolitan Region, Saudi Arabia, implicitly point to policy gaps by noting the “limited availability of public transportation” and the absence of “gender-responsive design and infrastructure.” They argue that road users should be encouraged to use sustainable mobility modes equally accessible to both men and women, implying that current policies do not adequately support this goal. The reliance on private vehicles, partly due to socio-cultural barriers, is also a consequence of policy failures to provide attractive and safe public transport alternatives for women.
Kacharo, Teshome, and Woltamo Kacharo et al. (2022) conclude their study on women’s safety in Hawassa city, Ethiopia, by stating that “cities must focus on developing gender-sensitive public transport service plans and policies that consider women and girls unique needs.” This is a direct call for policy intervention, recognizing that the current frameworks are not adequately addressing the violence and insecurity women face. Similarly, Infante-Vargas and Boyer Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021), after detailing the pervasiveness of GBV in Saltillo, Mexico, assert that “policy change is urgently needed in order to redress this problem.” This reinforces the idea that the current policy landscape is failing to protect women and facilitate their mobility.
Panjwani Panjwani (2018), in her policy paper on Karāchī, Pakistan, highlights “existing shortcomings in” and recommends “an urgent focus on a more inclusive, need-based public transportation system.” She draws on principles of gender mainstreaming interventions from other countries to make pragmatic recommendations, emphasizing inclusion, accessibility, safety, and mobility. This suggests that policy failures are often rooted in a lack of gender mainstreaming, where women’s needs are not integrated into the core of transport planning but are, at best, an afterthought.
Hortelano et al. Hortelano et al. (2021), in their European perspective, identify critical issues in the transport sector related to gender differences in accessibility, safety, and participation in research and innovation. They review EU policy initiatives but also assess progress and identify challenges, concluding with policy recommendations to overcome major barriers to gender equality. This indicates that even in regions with advanced policy frameworks, there are still significant gaps in achieving true gender equality in transport. Yasar and Basaran Yasar & Basaran (2025) also conclude their paper with policy recommendations to enhance security infrastructure while advancing gender equity in public transportation, underscoring the ongoing need for policy refinement and implementation.
Challenges in Data Collection and Institutional Sensitization
Effective policy-making is contingent upon robust data and a sensitized institutional environment, both of which are often lacking in the context of women’s mobility. Joshi et al. Joshi et al. (2022) specifically recommend “institutionalising gender-disaggregated data to inform interventions” and “sensitising institutions such as the law enforcement on gender equity and women’s rights to the city.” The absence of gender-disaggregated data means that women’s travel patterns, experiences of harassment, and specific needs are often invisible in transport planning, leading to generic solutions that fail to address their unique challenges. Without data, the extent of the problem remains unquantified, making it difficult to justify and design targeted interventions.
The lack of sensitization among institutions, particularly law enforcement and transport personnel, is another critical governance gap. If authorities are not adequately trained or aware of gender equity principles and women’s rights, incidents of harassment may go unreported, be dismissed, or even result in victim-blaming, as noted by Sil, Chowdhury, and Thoreau Sil et al. (2023). Kerzhner, Kaplan, and Silverman Kerzhner et al. (2018) suggest “immediate changes to symbology and behaviour of transport personnel” as a policy implication, recognizing that the attitudes and actions of those managing public transport can significantly impact women’s safety perceptions.
Thaher Thaher (2024), in the context of Amman, Jordan, emphasizes the need for strengthening data collection and management, and mitigating cultural biases in indicator selection, to achieve gender-sensitive sustainable urban transport planning. This highlights that data collection itself can be biased if not approached with a gender lens, and that cultural contexts must be considered to ensure data accurately reflects women’s experiences. Singh and Choudhury Singh & Choudhury (2025) also stress the need for “data-driven decision-making” and “gender- and age-sensitive urban transport policies” to ensure equitable access to mobility. Their proposed “Pink Map” tool is designed to leverage GIS mapping and real-time feedback, directly addressing the data gap and enabling more responsive planning.
International Guidelines vs. Local Implementation Realities
While international organizations often provide guidelines for developing inclusive public transport systems, their effective translation into national policies and local implementation remains a significant challenge. Sil, Chowdhury, and Thoreau Sil et al. (2023) explicitly mention this disparity, noting that despite international guidelines, women’s mobility remains restricted and unsafe in South and Southeast Asia. This suggests that national policy makers may face various obstacles, including lack of political will, insufficient resources, or a failure to adapt global recommendations to specific local contexts and socio-cultural realities.
Hortelano et al. Hortelano et al. (2021), in their European perspective, review EU policy initiatives and regulations that address gender equality and transport. While they acknowledge progress, they also identify ongoing challenges, indicating that even within a relatively harmonized policy environment, implementation gaps persist. This implies that policy is not a panacea; its effectiveness is determined by the commitment to its principles, the resources allocated for its execution, and the capacity of local authorities to translate it into tangible improvements on the ground.
The concept of “fairness” in transport, as discussed by Hail and McQuaid Hail & McQuaid (2021), provides a framework for understanding these policy gaps. They explore different concepts of fairness—equality of opportunity, equity, and justice—and argue that further research is required into setting frameworks for a more comprehensive inclusion and balance of these concepts in both transport policy and practice. This suggests that current policies may be failing to achieve true fairness for women because they are not adequately addressing the multiple dimensions of justice (procedural, social, distributional) in transport provision.
In conclusion, policy, planning, and governance gaps are fundamental barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit. The absence of gender-responsive policies, coupled with challenges in data collection, institutional sensitization, and the often-poor translation of international guidelines into local realities, creates a systemic environment that fails to prioritize and protect women’s right to safe and equitable mobility. Addressing these gaps requires a concerted effort to develop evidence-based, gender-sensitive policies, foster institutional capacity and awareness, and ensure robust implementation and accountability mechanisms.
Economic and Social Consequences of Restricted Mobility
The barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit extend far beyond mere inconvenience, imposing significant economic and social consequences that limit women’s opportunities, exacerbate inequalities, and hinder broader societal development. These consequences manifest in reduced labor market participation, constrained access to essential services, and diminished social engagement, ultimately undermining gender equity and sustainable urban development goals.
Impact on Labor Market Participation and Economic Opportunities
One of the most direct and profound consequences of restricted mobility for women is its detrimental impact on their labor market participation and economic opportunities. When women face unsafe, unreliable, or inaccessible public transport, their ability to commute to work, attend job interviews, or access training opportunities is severely curtailed. Field and Vyborny Field & Vyborny (2022) provide experimental evidence from Pakistan, demonstrating that reducing physical mobility constraints has a “large impact on job searching for women,” including those not searching at baseline. Their finding that a women-only transport treatment arm drove this response suggests that safety and social acceptability are key constraints, directly linking transport barriers to women’s economic engagement. This implies that if women cannot safely and acceptably reach workplaces, their labor supply is artificially restricted, limiting their economic independence and contributing to gender wage gaps.
Panjwani Panjwani (2018), in her analysis of Karāchī, Pakistan, explicitly links constraints to safe, efficient, and comfortable mobility to women’s “economic security.” She argues that the transportation sector’s gender discriminatory nature affects women’s mobility, which in turn is both a cause and an effect of social inequality. This highlights a vicious cycle where poor transport limits economic opportunities, reinforcing social inequality, which then perpetuates inadequate transport provision for women. The failure of federal budget allocation towards transportation, leading to profound gender disparities, further underscores how policy choices can exacerbate these economic consequences.
Sagaris and Tiznado-Aitken Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken (2020), examining Santiago, Chile, implicitly touch upon economic impacts by highlighting that barriers linked to unsafe public transport environments limit women’s mobility and, therefore, their participation. While “participation” can encompass various spheres, economic participation is a primary one. If women cannot reliably access work, their contribution to the formal economy is diminished, impacting household income and national productivity. Beck Beck (2024), focusing on women in the Metropolitan Region of Curitiba, Brazil, who use public transport to access domestic services work, directly illustrates this link. The limitations in accessibility, inclusion, and safety of public transport directly affect their ability to earn a livelihood, highlighting the vulnerability of women in low-wage sectors who are highly dependent on public transit.
The financial repercussions of unsafe public transport are also significant. Infante-Vargas and Boyer Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021), in their study on GBV in Saltillo, Mexico, argue that women can experience “financial and emotional repercussions” beyond the event of violence itself. These financial costs might include having to pay for more expensive private transport alternatives (e.g., taxis, ride-sharing) due to the perceived unsafety of public options, or the loss of income due to missed work days following a traumatic incident. Hail and McQuaid Hail & McQuaid (2021) also include cost as a significant fairness issue for women, especially those with low incomes, further demonstrating the economic burden imposed by inadequate public transport.
Constraints on Access to Essential Services and Social Life
Beyond the workplace, restricted mobility severely limits women’s access to a wide array of essential services, including healthcare, education, and social support networks, thereby impacting their overall well-being and social inclusion. King et al. King et al. (2021), in Bangladesh, note that equal access to safe transport is increasingly conceptualized as a fundamental right for women, with demonstrated impact on “health outcomes, social and economic mobility, and societal participation.” When women face multiple threats to their safety, they are forced to restrict their travel frequency, which inevitably affects their ability to access medical appointments, educational institutions, or community gatherings.
The complex travel patterns of women, often involving “trip chaining” for care-oriented purposes, mean that inadequate public transport disproportionately affects their ability to fulfill household and caregiving responsibilities. Sagaris and Tiznado-Aitken Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken (2020) emphasize the importance of considering non-work trip purposes and achieving better land-use combinations to accommodate these care-oriented trips. If public transport is unreliable or unsafe for these multi-stop journeys, women’s capacity to manage their daily lives, including taking children to school or accessing markets, is severely hampered. Vujičić et al. VUJIČIĆ et al. (2025)’s finding that women’s trips in Novi Sad, Serbia, are more related to daily duties than entertainment, scattered even outside peak hours, further underscores this point. When transit systems are not designed to accommodate these patterns, women’s ability to perform essential daily tasks is compromised.
The psychological impacts of fear and anxiety, as discussed by Kerzhner, Kaplan, and Silverman Kerzhner et al. (2018) and Roy, Bailey, and van Noorloos Roy et al. (2024), also contribute to social isolation. When women avoid certain areas or minimize travel due to fear, their social networks can shrink, and their participation in community life, cultural events, or recreational activities diminishes. This reduction in social engagement can have significant negative effects on mental health and overall quality of life, leading to a sense of despair and exclusion. Lindkvist Lindkvist (2024) notes that patriarchal norms “delimit women’s use and access to public space,” which inherently restricts their social life and public participation.
The Broader Implications for Gender Equity and Sustainable Development
Ultimately, the economic and social consequences of restricted mobility for women have broader implications for achieving gender equity and sustainable urban development. The inability of women to move freely and safely within cities perpetuates existing inequalities, hindering progress towards Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) related to gender equality (SDG 5), decent work and economic growth (SDG 8), and sustainable cities and communities (SDG 11).
Thaher Thaher (2024) argues that gender-sensitive sustainable urban transport planning is an “imperative rather than an aspiration” for promoting women’s well-being. He emphasizes that integrating women’s voices, needs, and preferences in planning processes is crucial for creating inclusive, equitable, and well-being-oriented urban transport systems. The study’s data from Amman, Jordan, underscores the need for interventions that break down barriers and amplify opportunities, thus transforming transportation networks into enablers of women’s empowerment and social participation. This perspective highlights that addressing mobility barriers is not just about transport but about fundamental human rights and societal progress.
Hail and McQuaid Hail & McQuaid (2021) discuss the concept of “fairness” in transport, arguing that women generally suffer disproportionate fairness issues. They emphasize that fairness principles should apply to all people, and that achieving vertical fairness (more disadvantaged people receiving greater support) is crucial. This implies that current transport systems, by failing women, are inherently unfair and contribute to broader societal injustices. Sagaris and Tiznado-Aitken Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken (2020) caution that environmental and economic sustainability gains in transport may be achieved at a “high social cost” unless specific measures are taken to address gender equity. For example, if women disproportionately account for walking trips due to lack of safe public transport, this might appear “sustainable” in terms of energy use but signifies significant inequalities in access. This critical perspective challenges a narrow view of sustainability that overlooks social equity.
Singh and Choudhury Singh & Choudhury (2025) reiterate that mobility plays a crucial role in ensuring social inclusion and economic participation. Their call for a multi-scalar approach integrating policy reforms, urban design strategies, and participatory planning is essential to achieving inclusive mobility for all, particularly women and the elderly. The overarching message from the literature is that addressing women’s mobility barriers in metro transit is not merely a technical transport problem but a complex socio-economic and political challenge with far-reaching implications for human rights, gender equality, and the creation of truly sustainable and equitable cities.
Methodological Approaches and Gaps in Understanding Women’s Mobility
The diverse and complex nature of barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit necessitates a variety of methodological approaches to fully capture their lived experiences, perceptions, and behavioral adaptations. The literature reviewed employs a mix of qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-methods designs, each offering unique strengths in uncovering different facets of the problem. However, these approaches also come with inherent limitations, pointing towards important research gaps and future directions.
Qualitative Insights into Lived Experiences
Qualitative research methods are particularly valuable for delving into the nuanced, subjective experiences of women, providing rich, in-depth understanding of their perceptions, emotions, and coping strategies. Several studies in this review effectively utilize qualitative approaches to illuminate the personal dimensions of mobility barriers.
Kerzhner, Kaplan, and Silverman Kerzhner et al. (2018), for instance, based their research on eight in-depth interviews and 38 participants in focus groups of Palestinian women in East Jerusalem. This approach allowed them to shed light on the “individual needs and decisions of women in making their travel choices in an exclusionary urban environment,” examining physical and psychological barriers. The qualitative data revealed how cultural and social pressures, exclusion, and fear pose significant limitations, and how women internalize expectations of violence. The strength of this method lies in its ability to uncover the “why” behind travel choices and the emotional toll of unsafe environments, which quantitative data alone might miss.
Roy, Bailey, and van Noorloos Roy et al. (2024) employed in-depth interviews and visual surveys to explore the “affects and emotions of everyday commutes” for women in Kolkata. By delving into the affective realm, they moved beyond simply identifying barriers to comprehending how emotions shape accessibility, acceptability, and affordability. This qualitative focus on emotions and affects provides a deeper understanding of the psychological burden women carry, revealing how disruptive affects like despair compel women to adjust or opt out of public transport. The visual surveys likely added another layer of rich, contextual data, allowing participants to express experiences that might be difficult to articulate verbally.
Joshi et al. Joshi et al. (2022) utilized data from in-depth interviews with women participants in two Indian cities (Delhi and Kolkata) to highlight the spectrum of barriers and the construction of safety through interventions. This qualitative approach allowed for a detailed exploration of women’s strategies, such as using technology or women-only solutions, and provided the basis for framing policy suggestions in a rights-based manner. Similarly, Lindkvist Lindkvist (2024)’s article is based on qualitative interviews exploring mobility practices, strategies, experiences with different modes, and reliability, affordability, and comfort of options in Sweden. This allowed her to uncover how safety concerns affect travel choices and reveal the persistence of patriarchal norms despite a reputation for gender equality.
Kacharo, Teshome, and Woltamo Kacharo et al. (2022), while primarily quantitative, incorporated 24 focus group discussion members, with data analyzed qualitatively using narrative analysis. This mixed approach allowed them to identify types of violence and factors contributing to it, complementing statistical findings with rich contextual narratives. Infante-Vargas and Boyer Infante-Vargas & Boyer (2021) also used a mixed-method approach, combining a survey with 11 interviews with transit users and a local women’s rights activist. The interviews provided crucial depth to understand the pervasiveness of GBV, re-victimization, and the psychological effects, enriching the quantitative survey data.
Beck Beck (2024) utilized the “Commented Walk” method to observe mobility conditions and challenges faced by women in the Metropolitan Region of Curitiba, Brazil. This immersive, participatory qualitative method allows researchers to experience and document mobility challenges firsthand alongside participants, offering unique insights into the physical and social environment of their journeys. The strength of these qualitative methods lies in their ability to capture the lived reality, subjective perceptions, and emotional dimensions of women’s mobility, which are crucial for understanding the full impact of barriers. However, their generalizability can be limited due to smaller sample sizes, necessitating complementary quantitative approaches.
Quantitative Analyses of Travel Behavior and Perceptions
Quantitative research methods provide statistical evidence, allowing for the identification of patterns, correlations, and the measurement of the prevalence and impact of various barriers across larger populations. These methods are essential for establishing the scale of the problem and informing policy at a broader level.
Pourhashem et al. Pourhashem et al. (2022) conducted an empirical analysis based on a multinomial regression model of 1406 female and 1486 male travelers in eight European countries. Their objective was to scrutinize gender differences in the perception of the value of travel time and factors influencing travel mode choice. This large-scale quantitative study allowed them to discover significant differences in perceived worthwhileness of travel time for public transport between female and male users, and how enjoyable travel time and perceived mood influence women’s tendency towards ridesharing or private motorized modes. The strength here is the ability to generalize findings across multiple countries and identify statistically significant gender differences in travel behavior.
Kacharo, Teshome, and Woltamo Kacharo et al. (2022) employed Binary Logistic Regression for quantitative data analysis from 199 randomly selected respondents in Ethiopia. This allowed them to identify six variables (age, marital status, type of public transport, travel time, facilities, and management) that significantly contributed to violence against women and girls. Such statistical analysis is crucial for identifying key risk factors and prioritizing interventions.
Al-Rashid et al. Al-Rashid et al. (2020) launched a survey campaign (structured interviews and online questionnaires) in the Dammam Metropolitan Region, Saudi Arabia, taking four different types of respondents into account. Their results, likely analyzed quantitatively, suggested a predominant preference for taxis due to poor public transport service. This large-scale survey approach provides a snapshot of preferences and service perceptions across a wider population, informing policy on service provision.
Vujičić et al. VUJIČIĆ et al. (2025) gathered results using travel diaries (collected by household surveys) in Novi Sad, Serbia, to analyze mobility, modal split, and distribution by travel reason as a function of gender. Their descriptive analysis revealed a greater prevalence of male employment and higher usage of passenger vehicles among men, while women used alternative modes and had more scattered, duty-related trips. This quantitative analysis of travel patterns is fundamental for understanding gender differences in mobility and informing urban planning.
Ali et al. Ali et al. (2025) used a mixed-method approach, including a survey of 583 randomly selected women commuters in Hyderabad, India. They employed two econometric models, binary logit and ordered probit, to analyze factors influencing transit safety and security at transit stops. This allowed them to statistically confirm the importance of infrastructural characteristics like CCTV and lighting, and sociodemographic factors like age and family opinion. The use of quantitative models provides robust evidence for policy recommendations.
Ceccato, Sundling, and Gliori Ceccato et al. (2024b) utilized exploratory data analysis and logistic regression models to assess patterns of victimization and safety perceptions among passengers using railway stations in Sweden. Their findings statistically showed how physical and social characteristics of transit environments affect safety perceptions and how women, particularly LGBTQI+/Non-binary/Other individuals, feel less safe. Their follow-up study Ceccato et al. (2024a) used chi-square analysis and regression models to explore women’s safety experiences in rural-urban contexts, statistically confirming that being young makes women more likely to be victimized and that rural women take more precautionary measures. These quantitative studies provide strong empirical backing for understanding the prevalence and determinants of safety concerns.
Thaher Thaher (2024) employed the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) methodology, surveying 38 stakeholders and decision-makers in Amman, Jordan. AHP is a structured technique for organizing and analyzing complex decisions, allowing for the quantification of stakeholder perceptions on accessibility, safety, and livability indicators. This quantitative approach helps prioritize interventions based on expert consensus, providing a systematic way to integrate gender perspectives into urban transport planning.
Limitations and Future Directions in Research Methodologies
While the diverse methodologies employed in the literature have significantly advanced the understanding of barriers to women’s mobility, several limitations and research gaps remain, pointing towards important future directions.
One key limitation is the potential for sampling bias in many studies. While some studies use random sampling (Ali et al., 2025; Kacharo et al., 2022), others rely on convenience or purposive sampling for qualitative work (Joshi et al., 2022; Kerzhner et al., 2018). While appropriate for in-depth insights, this can limit the generalizability of findings. Future research could benefit from larger, more representative samples, especially in diverse urban contexts, to ensure that the voices of all women, including those from marginalized communities, are heard.
Another gap lies in the longitudinal studies. Most studies provide a snapshot of current experiences. Longitudinal research could track changes in women’s mobility patterns, perceptions, and experiences over time, especially in response to specific policy interventions or infrastructural improvements. This would provide valuable insights into the effectiveness of different strategies and the dynamic nature of mobility barriers.
The intersectionality of gender with other social categories (e.g., age, disability, ethnicity, socio-economic status, sexual orientation, gender identity) is increasingly recognized as crucial, as highlighted by Ceccato, Sundling, and Gliori Ceccato et al. (2024b); Ceccato et al. (2024a). While some studies touch upon this, more dedicated research using intersectional frameworks is needed to understand how multiple forms of disadvantage compound women’s mobility barriers. This requires careful methodological design to ensure diverse voices are included and analyzed distinctly.
There is also a need for more comparative studies across different urban scales and contexts. While some studies offer regional insights (e.g., South Asia, Europe), direct comparisons using standardized methodologies across very different cultural and developmental contexts could reveal universal patterns and context-specific nuances more clearly. For instance, comparing the effectiveness of women-only solutions in conservative versus liberal societies, or the impact of technology in high-income versus low-income settings.
Furthermore, while technology is discussed as an intervention, there is a gap in evaluative research on the actual impact and effectiveness of specific technological solutions (e.g., safety apps, smart surveillance) on women’s perceived and actual safety in metro transit. Yasar and Basaran Yasar & Basaran (2025) highlight the need to assess the effectiveness of in-vehicle security applications in promoting equitable transit access, and the potential future impacts of autonomous vehicles. Rigorous evaluations, perhaps using quasi-experimental designs, are needed to understand which interventions truly work and why.
Finally, the integration of diverse data sources could be further enhanced. Singh and Choudhury Singh & Choudhury (2025) propose the “Pink Map” leveraging GIS mapping, community participation, and real-time feedback. Such innovative approaches that combine spatial data, qualitative narratives, and quantitative metrics can provide a more holistic and actionable understanding of women’s mobility challenges. Future research should explore how to effectively integrate “big data” from transit systems (e.g., ridership patterns, incident reports) with qualitative insights and survey data to create comprehensive analytical frameworks.
In conclusion, the methodological landscape for studying women’s mobility barriers is rich and varied, offering powerful tools for understanding this complex issue. However, by addressing existing limitations and embracing innovative, interdisciplinary, and intersectional approaches, future research can provide even more robust evidence to inform the development of truly inclusive and equitable metro transit systems worldwide.
Conclusion
The comprehensive review of the literature on barriers to women’s mobility in metro transit reveals a complex and pervasive global challenge, deeply rooted in a confluence of safety concerns, infrastructural deficiencies, socio-cultural norms, and policy gaps. Women worldwide disproportionately experience public transport as a space of vulnerability, fear, and restriction, rather than one of empowerment and access.
The most prominent barrier identified across diverse geographical contexts is the pervasive issue of safety and security, primarily manifesting as gender-based violence and harassment (Ali et al., 2025; Ceccato et al., 2024b, 2024a; Infante-Vargas & Boyer, 2021; Joshi et al., 2022; Kacharo et al., 2022; Kerzhner et al., 2018; King et al., 2021; Lindkvist, 2024; Roy et al., 2024; Sil et al., 2023; Yasar & Basaran, 2025). This threat is not merely physical but also profoundly psychological, instilling fear, anxiety, and despair that compel women to alter their travel patterns, restrict their destinations, or avoid public transport altogether (Infante-Vargas & Boyer, 2021; Kerzhner et al., 2018; Roy et al., 2024). The psychological burden of constant vigilance and the internalization of perceived risks significantly diminish women’s spatial freedom and autonomy.
Infrastructural deficiencies and poor service quality constitute another critical set of barriers. These include inadequate design and maintenance of stations and vehicles, lack of gender-responsive infrastructure, overcrowding, unreliability, limited operational routes and hours, and a mismatch with women’s complex, multi-purpose trip chaining patterns (Ali et al., 2025; Al-Rashid et al., 2020; Kerzhner et al., 2018; Panjwani, 2018; Roy et al., 2024; Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken, 2020; Sil et al., 2023; Singh & Choudhury, 2025; VUJIČIĆ et al., 2025). These shortcomings not only make travel inconvenient but also directly compromise women’s safety and comfort, often forcing them into more expensive or less sustainable private transport alternatives.
Deeply entrenched socio-cultural and patriarchal norms form an underlying layer of barriers, shaping women’s roles, restricting their spatial freedom, and influencing their interactions in public spaces (Al-Rashid et al., 2020; Beck, 2024; Field & Vyborny, 2022; Lindkvist, 2024; Sil et al., 2023; Thaher, 2024). These norms can lead to social unacceptability of women in certain public transport contexts, contribute to victim-blaming, and foster an environment where harassment is tacitly condoned. The intersectionality of gender with other social factors such as age, ethnicity, socio-economic status, and gender identity further compounds these challenges, creating unique vulnerabilities for different groups of women (Ceccato et al., 2024b, 2024a; Hail & McQuaid, 2021; Kerzhner et al., 2018).
Finally, significant policy, planning, and governance gaps perpetuate these barriers. The absence of adequate gender-responsive transport policies, insufficient institutional sensitization, lack of gender-disaggregated data, and a disconnect between international guidelines and local implementation realities are consistently highlighted (Ali et al., 2025; Al-Rashid et al., 2020; Hortelano et al., 2021; Infante-Vargas & Boyer, 2021; Joshi et al., 2022; Kacharo et al., 2022; Panjwani, 2018; Sil et al., 2023; Singh & Choudhury, 2025; Thaher, 2024; Yasar & Basaran, 2025). These systemic failures mean that women’s specific needs and vulnerabilities are often overlooked in transport planning, leading to systems that are inherently exclusionary.
The economic and social consequences of these restricted mobilities are profound, impacting women’s labor market participation, economic security, access to education and healthcare, and overall social inclusion (Beck, 2024; Field & Vyborny, 2022; Infante-Vargas & Boyer, 2021; King et al., 2021; Panjwani, 2018; Sagaris & Tiznado-Aitken, 2020; Thaher, 2024). This ultimately hinders progress towards broader gender equity and sustainable urban development goals.
Research Gaps and Future Directions: While the existing literature provides a robust foundation, several research gaps and future directions emerge: 1. Longitudinal Studies: Most studies offer a snapshot. Longitudinal research is needed to track changes in women’s mobility experiences over time, especially in response to specific policy or infrastructural interventions, providing insights into their long-term effectiveness. 2. Intersectional Analysis: While acknowledged, more dedicated research employing intersectional frameworks is required to understand how gender intersects with other social categories (e.g., age, disability, ethnicity, sexual orientation, gender identity) to create compounded mobility barriers. 3. Evaluative Research on Interventions: There is a need for rigorous evaluative studies on the actual impact and effectiveness of specific interventions, such as women-only transport, safety apps, or improved lighting, across diverse contexts. This includes understanding their unintended consequences and whether they truly address root causes or merely offer temporary fixes. 4. Comparative Studies: More comparative research using standardized methodologies across vastly different cultural and developmental contexts could reveal universal patterns and context-specific nuances more clearly, informing globally applicable yet locally sensitive solutions. 5. Data Integration and Innovative Methodologies: Future research should explore innovative methodologies that integrate diverse data sources, including “big data” from transit systems, GIS mapping, qualitative narratives, and real-time feedback, as exemplified by the “Pink Map” concept (Singh & Choudhury, 2025). This will provide a more holistic and actionable understanding of women’s mobility challenges. 6. Focus on Policy Implementation and Governance: Deeper investigation into the political economy of transport planning is needed to understand why gender-responsive policies are often lacking or poorly implemented, and how governance structures can be reformed to prioritize women’s mobility needs.
In conclusion, achieving truly inclusive and equitable metro transit systems for women requires a multi-faceted approach that addresses safety, infrastructure, socio-cultural norms, and policy gaps simultaneously. It demands a fundamental shift in perspective, moving from gender-neutral planning to gender-sensitive and transformative approaches that place women’s diverse needs and rights at the center of urban mobility development.
