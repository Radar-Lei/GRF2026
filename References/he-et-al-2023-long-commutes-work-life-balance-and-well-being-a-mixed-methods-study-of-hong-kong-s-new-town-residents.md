# Long Commutes, Work-Life Balance, and Well-Being: A Mixed-Methods Study of Hong Kong’s New-Town Residents

Sylvia Y. He1 , Xueying Chen2, and Sui Tao3

# Abstract

This study examines the cascading effects of long commutes on the work-life balance and subjective well-being of commuters from Hong Kong’s new towns. We collected questionnaire surveys and developed a structural equation model. Our results show that new-town commuters experienced lower levels of travel satisfaction, work-life balance, and subjective well-being. Meanwhile, our qualitative analysis of interview transcripts reveals that new-town residents endure longer commutes for various reasons but primarily to enjoy the better natural environment and living conditions available in suburban areas. However, the inadequate supply of public amenities and social and cultural venues in the suburbs can undermine well-being.

# Introduction

The development of new towns has been considered a key strategy for decentralizing growing populations in urban centers while mitigating urban problems, including rising rent costs and traffic congestion (Alexander 2009; Hall 2014). With roots in the “garden cities” envisioned by Ebenezer Howard (1898) and the English New Town Movement (Hall 2014), establishing self-contained communities has been considered among the core goals of new town development (Cervero 1995a). In the past, we have witnessed certain new towns successfully attract both population and jobs (Forsyth 2019; Rowe 2021). Unfortunately, enshrining adequate job opportunities in new towns is often not sufficiently prioritized or is difficult to achieve in new town planning practices (He, Tao, et al. 2020; He, Wu, et al. 2020; Yang and Day 2015b). Some new towns have not experienced significant increases in job opportunities, even decades after their establishment (He, Wu, et al. 2020).

The inadequacies of local economies and the jobs-housing imbalance in new towns imply long commutes to activity centers (Cervero 1995b; Cervero and Wu 1998). Extensive research has revealed that long commutes can lead to lower levels of travel satisfaction (De Vos et al. 2013; Mao, Ettema, and Dijst 2016) and have cascading effects on nonwork activities, impacting work-life balance (WLB). For example, lower job accessibility not only lengthens commutes for new-town residents but also reduces the time they spend on discretionary and maintenance trips on workdays (He, Tao, et al. 2020). Prolonged commutes might also reduce the window of time and opportunity for engaging in joint household and other recreational activities, meaning new-town commuters engage in fewer joint household activities than their urban area counterparts (Tao and He 2021). The lack of adequate job opportunities in new towns may also affect the subjective well-being (SWB) of individuals (e.g., Dickerson et al. 2014). For example, long commutes may produce psychological burdens and stress that can negatively impact both the physical and mental health of commuters (e.g., Wang et al. 2019; Yang and Day 2015a). Previous research has largely considered the long commute’s effect on travel satisfaction (Mao, Ettema, and Dijst 2016), with some studies investigating the impact on WLB or SWB (He, Tao, et al. 2020; Sirgy and Lee 2018; Wheatley 2014). However, few studies have considered the interrelationship between travel satisfaction, WLB, and SWB, especially regarding the interactions between the latter two covariates. This paper intends to fill this research gap. Furthermore, our study leverages not only statistical models to quantify the relationship but also a qualitative approach designed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential trade-offs of long commutes for new-town residents.

Our study context is Hong Kong, a postcolonial region that has closely followed Britain’s new-town development model. We collected first-hand data using a questionnaire survey and in-depth interviews with residents from urban and new-town areas in 2020. For the quantitative analysis, we built a structural equation model (SEM) to examine the effects of commute duration on WLB and SWB and compare the differences between urban and new-town residents. We found that longer commute duration was indeed associated with living in new towns, which exerted indirect negative effects on the WLB and SWB of commuters via its impact on travel satisfaction. For the qualitative analysis, we used thematic analysis methods to further disclose the perceived pros and cons of living in new towns for commuters. We found that although new-town dwellers tended to be dissatisfied with long commutes, they were compensated by other quality-of-life aspects, including better living conditions and proximity to open space. Nevertheless, new-town residents were less satisfied with the suburban public amenities and venues for social and cultural activities, which calls for further improvement.

# Commuting, WLB, and SWB

Extensive research on commuting has focused on elucidating the correlations between daily commutes and spatial attributes, particularly job accessibility (Levinson 1998). Empirical findings have associated greater accessibility to jobs with shorter commute distances or durations, conditional upon specific social-spatial contexts and levels of motorization $\mathrm { H u } \ 2 0 1 5$ ; Kawabata and Shen 2007; P. Zhu et al. 2020).

In recent years, we have also witnessed increasing attention to the impact of daily commutes on WLB and SWB. It has been revealed that commute duration can negatively impact WLB, with longer commutes reducing the window for engagement in noncommute activities (Bai et al. 2021; He, Tao, et al. 2020) and time spent with family (Tao and He 2021). Meanwhile, commute duration can significantly impact commuter SWB. For example, J. Zhu and Fan (2018) revealed a linkage between commute duration and the financial and psychological burdens of daily travel. Prolonged commutes may also produce negative feelings, such as distress and anxiety, further impacting individual well-being (De Vos et al. 2013).

However, the relationship between commuting, WLB, and SWB is less straightforward than it might seem: commuters endure long commutes for complex reasons, and some commuters may not consider their long commute a negative. Research using qualitative approaches (e.g., focus group interviews) has revealed that people might consider commute time enjoyable if it serves as a transition between work and family life (Jain and Lyons 2008; Watts and Urry 2008). Commuters may “use the time for relaxing or thinking, including ‘shifting gears’ mentally between the origin and destination activities and roles” (Mokhtarian and Salomon 2001, 702).

Furthermore, traveling longer distances to work might represent a trade-off, enabling commuters to obtain other benefits (e.g., Lyons and Chatterjee 2008). From an urban economic perspective, the suburbs (including new towns) tend to represent lower rents and housing prices, larger living spaces, and newer amenities than the older urban areas (O’Sullivan 2009). From a household perspective, workers may choose to endure longer commutes in exchange for shorter commutes for other household members (e.g., Schwanen and Mokhtarian 2005). In each case, the “benefit” eclipses the downsides of the commuting experience.

However, despite the accumulation of studies on commuting, WLB, and SWB, there remain various research gaps. First, research explicitly analyzing the interrelationship between commuting, WLB, and SWB has been limited, especially commuting’s simultaneous interface with WLB and SWB (Clark 2000). Second, there are inconclusive findings regarding the effects of commuting on WLB and SWB, demanding additional empirical evidence. Third, the literature on the relationship between commuting and WLB has mainly used quantitative analyses. Fourth, few studies on long commutes have focused on new towns, despite new towns becoming Asia’s major form of urban expansion and development (Forsyth 2021), introducing concerns about long commutes and jobs-housing imbalance for various reasons (He, Tao, et al. 2020). Accordingly, this study adopts a mixed-methods approach to enhance understanding of the interrelationships between commute duration, WLB, and SWB in the new-town context.

# Research Design: Study Area, Data Collection, and Methodology

Study Area and Data Collection

The study area of Hong Kong has a population of over 7.3 million and covers a land area of over $1 { , } 1 0 0 \mathrm { k m } ^ { 2 }$ . It consists of three main regions: Hong Kong Island (HKI), the Kowloon Peninsula (Kowloon), and the New Territories (NT). HKI and Kowloon are the main urban areas of Hong Kong. Meanwhile, NT encompasses the new towns and represents suburban areas characterized by lower population and built up densities (Figure 1). Hong Kong is also highly transit dependent, with over 70 percent of daily motorized trips made by public transport (particularly bus and Mass Transit Railway [MTR]) (Hong Kong Transport Department 2014).

Since the late 1970s, Hong Kong has developed new towns in the NT to critically alleviate the pressure associated with the rapid population growth in the urban areas of HKI and Kowloon. The 2016 census indicates that the NT population exceeds 3.8 million. Although providing adequate employment opportunities has been among the key goals of new town planning, it has not been well implemented (He, Tao, et al. 2020). Instead, the consequent lack of local job opportunities demands that many new-town residents commute long distances every day, undermining their WLB (He, Tao, et al. 2020). The high concentration of public housing projects in new towns may have contributed to this imbalance between employment and housing (Yeh 2004). Notably, Hong Kong’s public housing is heavily subsidized by the government. With prices and rents much lower than the market rates, public housing aims to accommodate the housing demand of the low-income population. Accordingly, public housing residents also tend to be less socioeconomically mobile and less likely to work and live in the same place due to limited choice of residential location and workplace (Lui and Suen 2011). Notably, the city is currently cultivating several new development areas, many of which are adjacent to the existing new towns (e.g., Kwu Tung near Fanling as well as Hung Shui Kiu near Yuen Long). This means that this study’s findings and implications not only concern previously implemented new-town development practices but also offer contemporary guidelines for ongoing projects in new development areas.

This study’s questionnaire surveys and semi-structured interviews were both conducted at sixteen sites (Figure 1) across Hong Kong between January and June 2020. Study sites were selected based on their proximity to transit hubs (e.g., MTR stations) and popular community facilities (e.g., shopping malls) within the corresponding new town or urban district. Researchers and assistants worked in pairs to recruit participants using a systematic random sampling procedure. For every five people they met, researchers approached and invited one person to participate in the survey. During recruitment, filter questions were employed to identify commuters aged between eighteen and sixty-five years and working full time. We built upon previous studies to develop survey instruments and an interview protocol (Abou-Zeid and BenAkiva 2011; Greenhaus et al. 2003; White 1999). Appendix A summarizes the survey instruments.

The survey comprised four sections: (1) commute characteristics and satisfaction (e.g., travel mode and commute time); (2) WLB (e.g., work performance, involvement in family activities); (3) SWB measurement; and (4) personal information. To enhance internal validity, a pilot study was performed before conducting the main round of fieldwork. Based on the feedback, the questionnaire was revised to improve the interpretability of the questions. A total of 316 valid samples were collected, including 240 from the NT (twelve sites) and 76 from urban areas (forty-six from two sites in Kowloon and thirty from two sites on HKI). The size of our population—employed workers in Hong Kong—was approximately 3.2 million (excluding foreign domestic helpers) in 2022 (Census and Statistics Department 2022). After excluding cases with missing and erroneous information, the final sample size of 296 could reach a 90-percent confidence level with a 5-percent margin of error.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted immediately after participants completed the questionnaire survey. Specifically, participants were asked if they were willing to participate in a semi-structured follow-up interview that would last a maximum of fifteen to twenty minutes. A total of 117 interviews were conducted, with key questions including “How do you perceive your journey to work and your well-being in general?” and “Is living in a neighborhood served by public transport important to you and your family and in which ways?” Thematic analysis identified common themes, revealing that work arrangements, life cycles, and lifestyles differed according to commute experience, WLB, and SWB.

# Measuring Travel Satisfaction

We employed two items to measure the level of travel satisfaction regarding commuting trips: “I enjoy the trips to and from the workplace” and “All things considered, I am satisfied with my commute from home to work” (Abou-Zeid and Ben-Akiva 2011). These two items were assessed using a 7-point Likert-type scale, where 1 signaled “strongly disagree” and 7 signaled “strongly agree.” The Cronbach’s alpha for the two items was .83.

# Statistical Models

SEM was used to examine the interrelationships conceptualized in Figure 2. SEM is particularly useful for research concerned with observed and latent variables (e.g., attitudinal factors) and complicated interrelationships (Byrne 2001; Golob 2003). SEM involves simultaneously estimating regression coefficients and residuals based on the variancecovariance matrix of the observed variables. # Qualitative Analysis

To explore the interview data, we conducted a thematic analysis to identify common themes, patterns, and relationships via the following steps. First, we studied each interview transcript and identified potential themes. This led to open coding to generate categories and group those categories within high-order headings for each transcript (Burnard 1991). Second, we highlighted potential keywords based on the frequency count. Third, we created a master code list and reexamined the transcripts against the list of categories and high-frequency words to identify key themes and sentiments related to participants’ commute experience and well-being.

# SEM Estimation

We estimated an SEM model to reveal the relationship between commuting, WLB, and SWB. Figure 3 shows the SEM results. The model fit was reasonable $( \chi ^ { 2 } = 4 6 7 . 6 9 , p$ $< . 0 0 1$ , $d f = 2 9 2$ , $\chi ^ { 2 } / d f = 1 . 6 0 2$ , comparative fit index [CFI] $= 0 . 9 4 6$ , root mean square error of approximation [RMSEA] $= \ 0 . 0 4 5 )$ , and the coefficients of the key variables largely aligned with our expectations. Using public transit increased commute duration, especially for new-town residents. This suggests that longer commutes were indeed associated with using public transit and living in new towns. Both commute duration and public transport usage negatively impacted travel satisfaction, with both variables consequently exerting negative impacts on WLB and the three SWB dimensions. In addition, greater travel satisfaction was linked to better WLB, which, in turn, related positively to SWB.

However, although living in new towns significantly affected commute duration, it had no statistically significant direct impact on travel satisfaction or WLB, largely because the effect of new towns on these two outcome variables was mediated (or captured) by commute duration. Similarly, the indirect effects of new towns on WLB and SWB were small and statistically insignificant, largely mediated by commute duration and travel satisfaction. This finding provides insights into how living in a new town can affect a commuter’s WLB and SWB.

The sociodemographic variables (i.e., gender, age, income level, educational attainment, and housing type) were control variables in our SEM model. A series of tests revealed that several variables insignificantly impacted the key outcome variables (i.e., commuting duration, travel satisfaction, and WLB). As such, they were excluded to obtain a more parsimonious modeling outcome. Notably, the results suggest that higher incomes might provide more resources, enabling a better balancing of work and family life, with dependent children potentially (to some extent) compelling commuters to pay particular attention to WLB. However, older adults and those living in public housing might experience more barriers or difficulties balancing work and family life (see Appendix D for more details).

# Results from the Qualitative Analysis

For our in-depth interviews, a total of 117 interviews were conducted and transcribed. The general profile of the interview participants provided a relatively good representation of Hong Kong’s full-time workers. Among our interview participants, 62 percent were male, and 52 percent were married; these numbers were 58 and 51 percent in the 2016 census. In terms of age, those aged forty-six to sixty-five years were underrepresented compared to the census.

Word frequency analysis of the interview transcriptions enabled the identification of keywords related to participant travel experiences, WLB, and SWB. The most frequent word was “(in)convenient” in the context of certain travel circumstances. This probably concerned access to transit services: it appears that having easy access to transit services in the neighborhood represents an important indicator of convenience (or lack thereof) for both commute and noncommute trips. More specifically, convenience may entail time-saving related to using transit services and the concentration of various services and facilities around transit stations (especially MTR stations). Additionally, transit stations often serve as meeting points for friends and family members, potentially attributable to their highly recognizable locations or their function as major meeting places (He et al. 2018). Several participants also used “convenient” to express their desire for better access to different transit options and to other leisure and social activities (e.g., dining and shopping).

When participants discussed their experience of commuting and their related attitudes (in response to the questions “How would you perceive your journey to work and your well-being in general?” and “Ideally, how long would you expect to spend commuting for work each way?”), some participants stated that longer commutes had negative emotional and physical impacts (e.g., experiencing feelings of tiredness and stress). We also found that “30 minutes” was frequently suggested as the ideal longest commute time, regardless of residential location. Alongside identifying “30 minutes” as an acceptable threshold for commute time, participants indicated that the remaining time in the day should be used for other purposes, including spending time with family and friends or engaging in personal hobbies, which could enhance SWB. However, for some respondents, the commute experience was more important than the temporal duration. Longer commutes can be endured if the experience is comfortable (e.g., having a seat, no transfers), reliable, and predictable (e.g., punctual and limited congestion). Furthermore, some respondents characterized new towns as featuring a “better environment,” with more green space, lower rents, and lower occupancy densities, which were considered major advantages over living in urban areas. For example, one participant expressed her limited concerns about her long commute as follows:

Thirty minutes each way would be ideal, but I do not feel strongly about this [long commute], because it is easy to get a seat on the bus when traveling from here. And it is much quieter [than the city center], [and] I like quiet environments. (E, female, thirty to forty years, administrative support staff, lives in Kwai Chung [new town])

When participants reflected on the relationship (including the trade-offs) between daily commuting and living costs, they frequently mentioned another keyword: “affordable.” Some participants opted for transit modes (e.g., minibus) that were more “affordable” despite being more time-consuming. They also recognized that shops in certain new towns (e.g., Sheung Shui/Fanling) were more affordable than those in the urban core areas. Additionally, many new-town commuters acknowledged that they endured longer commutes due to the housing affordability problem. For example, according to one respondent,

I no longer feel that an hour each way bothers me, because I am used to it. You know, housing is not a free choice in Hong Kong. I want to own an apartment in the city, but I can only afford housing here in Tai Po. (J, male, father of two children, fifty to sixty years, lives in Tai Po [new town])

Another new-town participant expressed a similar perspective on affordability in suburban living: “I would like to move closer to my job, but it is too expensive to live in the city, and I cannot afford it. I live with my parents, and I do not need to pay rent” (C, female, twenty to twenty-five years, fresh graduate, finance industry, lives in Tai Po [new town]). Several other respondents echoed this sentiment, highlighting that housing and relocation costs were too high for them to reside closer to their workplace. This suggests that the imbalance between jobs and housing is a pronounced issue in Hong Kong and one that is compounded by the high housing prices in urban areas (He 2020). The trade-off between commuting and lower living cost suggests that new-town residents are, to some extent, compensated for their long commute by affordable housing in the suburbs (e.g., Day and Cervero 2010).

# Thematic Analysis

According to the thematic analysis of our interviews, there are different types of commuting patterns. For commuters from new towns, the most common pattern is commuting to urban areas for work. The proximity to the natural environment and better living conditions makes the long commute worth it. There are also commuters who commute within the same new town. In addition, we found cases of reverse commutes, which describes urban residents traveling to new towns for work while preferring the public services that their urban neighborhood offers.

In summary, we identified five main themes from the interviews:

1. Commute experience varies, and it influences WLB and SWB;   
2. Suburban residents endure longer commutes to have access to a better living environment, but the commute experience may vary between new towns;   
3. Long commutes can be caused by household considerations;   
4. Reverse commuting exists in the Hong Kong context;   
5. Shorter commutes do not necessarily equate to higher WLB and SWB if noncommute travel is inconvenient.

Commute experience varies, and it influences WLB and SWB. Aligning with the extant literature, our results reveal that commuters have different experiences and attitudes toward commuting. In general, participants indicate that the commuting experience would be more “enjoyable” and “relaxing” if there were few or no transfers, on-time transit services, adequate seats on board, and the ability to multitask. By contrast, commuters generally dislike long waiting times, low service frequencies, and extreme weather conditions. In particular, the first and last mile between an individual’s home and workplace and their transit station could significantly influence their mood for the day, with one participant explaining that commuting remained stressful despite living within a fifteen-minute walk of his workplace:

It is fine to walk 15 minutes [to my workplace] each way. However, it becomes stressful if you must walk, rain or shine, every day, particularly during [the] summers or when storms occur. There is no covered sidewalk, and I have no options other than walking. (F, male, twenty to thirty years, finance industry, lives and works in Tin Shui Wai [new town])

Suburban residents endure longer commutes to have access to a better living environment, but the commute experience may vary between new towns. The interviews suggested that there are various advantages and disadvantages associated with living in new towns compared to living in urban areas (summarized in Appendix E). Although residents of urban areas indicated that good access to public transit and other facilities was beneficial for their daily lives, some admitted that noise pollution, overcrowdedness, and aging apartments could negatively impact their quality of life. Meanwhile, interviewees from the Sha Tin, Tsuen Wan, and Kwai Chung new towns agreed that proximity to urban areas meant that their communities generally had good access to shops and public services. In contrast, commuters in remote new towns (e.g., Fanling/ Sheung Shui and Tung Chung) complained that their access to public transit and other public facilities was limited. To some extent, the different commute experiences reported by different new-town residents reflect previous findings regarding the differences in commute times and independence indices across the NT (He, Tao, et al. 2020).

Long commutes can be caused by household considerations. We found that certain commuters might endure longer commutes for the well-being of their families. For example, some participants were willing to undertake longer commutes to enable family members to live in their preferred neighborhood, which they considered important for the whole family’s well-being. In one example, a new-town participant explained his situation as follows:

I live with my parents, who retired already. They prefer the green space and the air quality in new towns. As long as my parents are happy in the current neighborhood, it is fine for me to commute [a] long distance. (Z, male, thirty to forty years, lives in Tai Po [new town] and works in Kowloon)

Reverse commuting exists in the Hong Kong context. The interviews also revealed the existence of the reverse commuting phenomenon in the Hong Kong context. Some workers who found jobs in new towns continued to live in urban areas. Many indicated that they have better access to public services in their urban neighborhoods (e.g., schools and libraries). For example, a father of two children who worked in Shatin in the NT and lived on HKI commuted about an hour each way. He indicated that he did not relocate to be closer to his job because his children “have better options for schools in the city” (K, male, forty to fifty years, IT professional, lives on HKI). The willingness to travel to gain access to education resources reflects the findings of previous studies concerning transport and urban economics (e.g., He and Giuliano 2018). Meanwhile, in our study, the reverse commute also reflected the spatial discrepancy between public amenities and social infrastructure (e.g., schools and cultural events) in urban areas and suburban new towns.

Shorter commutes do not necessarily equate to higher WLB and SWB $i f$ noncommute travel is inconvenient. Although some participants experienced relatively short commutes by working in the same new town, their remote residential location can imply a long distance from the city center and social activities. Participants indicated that having reasonably good access to both public transit and social and leisure opportunities is important for their well-being. For example, according to a participant from Tung Chung who had relocated from an urban area,

I relocated here for my job. But occasionally, I feel I am left out from the world, being isolated [in Tung Chung]. I tried once to hang out with friends and family after work, and it took me almost three hours [to get] to and back from the city [center] that night. . .The trip took too much time, and I was exhausted. Since then, I have refused to participate in any other social activities with my friends, except occasionally [on] weekends. (R, female, thirty to forty years, works at the airport and lives in Tung Chung [new town])

# Discussion and Conclusion

Seventy-five years after the English New Town Movement, the practice of new town development persists, and scholarly interest remains strong (e.g., Peiser and Forsyth 2021). Building on the seminal works in the literature on planning that focus on commuting time and independence indices (e.g., Cervero 1995a, 1995b), we are witnessing increasing attention on long commutes. In the context of Hong Kong’s new towns (in comparison to the city’s urban areas), this study has examined the interrelationships between commuting experience, WLB, and SWB using a mixed-methods approach.

Combining a questionnaire survey with interview data proved useful for revealing complementary insights. The quantitative analysis detected that new-town residents tended to endure longer commutes, which exhibited a cascading effect on travel satisfaction, WLB, and SWB. Given that the longer commutes of new-town residents have also been observed in the peripheral areas of certain transit-dependent cities (Zhao et al. 2020; Zhou, Wu, and Cheng 2013), we expect similarly negative effects of longer commutes on WLB and SWB to appear in other transit-dependent cities and jobdeficient suburban areas. Meanwhile, the findings from the qualitative analysis provide insights into the complex interconnections between commuting and an individual’s quality of life. To reduce the total journey time from the suburbs to the city center (i.e., the workplace), commuters in new towns highly valued proximity to public transit, especially the region’s main urban rail system (the MTR). Meanwhile, although our interviews revealed that new-town residents benefited from the better living environment and housing conditions in the NT, certain new-town commuters were concerned about the limited access to public amenities, especially the possible effect on their social life and well-being. This finding suggests that Hong Kong new towns lack not only job opportunities but also public amenities and social and cultural venues. This implies that the vision of self-contained new towns has not been fully achieved in the case of Hong Kong.

Some implications can be derived to inform the spatial planning of Hong Kong and potentially other similar urban contexts. First, our study again cautions against new town planning that overlooks promoting the balance between jobs and housing and local economy agglomeration, even within high-density contexts. In such cases, measures can be considered to reduce long commutes and the associated burdens (e.g., telecommuting and flexible working hours). Such measures might improve commuting efficiency and individual well-being and reduce traffic congestion during peak hours around the region’s bottlenecks. In addition, the latest Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (SAR) Chief Executive’s Policy Address in 2021 proposed the development of the Northern Metropolis, which would not only increase the residential population by more than one million but would also create approximately four hundred fifty thousand jobs in an area that encompasses several new towns (Tin Shui Wai, Yuen Long, and Fanling/Sheung Shui), New Development Areas and Development Nodes, as well as neighboring rural areas in Yuen Long District and North District. Probably for the first time, the address explicitly acknowledged the inadequacies of Hong Kong’s new town planning in achieving “home-job balance” and “self-sufficiency,” a planning issue that the government intends to take proactive steps to redress (The Government of Hong Kong 2021). Second, our study has associated longer commutes with lower housing prices in a way that represents vulnerable social strata, which basically aligns with the findings of certain previous studies (e.g., Grengs 2010; Zhou, Wu, and Cheng 2013). More particularly, measures should be taken to improve the commuting experience and increase travel satisfaction among public transport commuters in new towns (or remote suburbs). Meanwhile, the lack of access to other public facilities remains a major challenge for some remote new towns. Hence, establishing high-quality public amenities and cultural venues in new towns could reduce the need for long-distance travel for noncommuting trips. Finally, this study provides a framework that can be used to assess the social impacts of other new-town projects and new development areas, helping to better incorporate consideration of enhancing quality of life into spatial planning.

However, this study features certain limitations. One possible limitation pertains to the data collection locations (near transit hubs and in parks adjacent to shopping centers), which might have reduced the likelihood of approaching car users. However, given that less than 10 percent of employees commute by private car in Hong Kong (Hong Kong Transport Department 2014), we believe that this had a negligible impact on the research outcomes. Another potential limitation is that the impact of nonwork accessibility (Tao et al. 2023) on WLB and SWB was not captured by our questionnaire survey, although we explored these factors to some extent in the indepth interviews. Future research should examine the impact of nonwork opportunities on WLB and SWB and whether proximity to such facilities may mitigate the negative effects of long commutes for new-town residents. Furthermore, although the survey was carried out when the COVID pandemic was relatively under control, pandemic-era effects might still have impacted the findings.1 However, we were unable to fully control for such effects in this study. Future research may address this limitation by performing new rounds of data collection in the postpandemic era. Finally, our study did not capture the impact of self-selection on the reported well-being of participating new-town residents. Considering this influence may provide more nuanced insights into the relationship between residential status and well-being (Day 2013). Future research could address this limitation by conducting more comprehensive surveys.

This paper contributes to the new-town development literature by offering empirical evidence for strategies aimed at improving the commuting experience and quality of life of new-town residents. We believe that our findings enhance our understanding of how new towns (and potentially other suburban forms) can be better developed to improve travel satisfaction, WLB, and SWB. The study’s findings also provide useful information for policymakers and planners engaged in new town development, facilitating more sustainable urban growth. Finally, although our research focuses on new towns in Hong Kong, we believe that our findings and the implications offer general insights that relate to similar issues faced by job-deficient suburbs in other contexts.
